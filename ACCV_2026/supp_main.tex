\section{From-Scratch Training: An Out-of-Distribution Probe}
\label{supp:designcut}

The main analysis studies each backbone matcher (CATs++, GLU-Net, FlowFormer)
with its pretrained image-feature backbone, and RAFT (which has no such
backbone) end-to-end (main Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}). This section documents in full the from-scratch
configurations of the three backbone matchers, in which the feature extractors are
randomly initialized. We treat them as out-of-distribution for two
reasons, established below: (i) removing pretrained features does \emph{not} make
the model lean on appearance (an appearance distance gains no predictive power
from scratch), so the probe's motivating hypothesis fails; and (ii) the
randomly-initialized backbones mode-collapse to a near-static prior that ignores
the image pair, so no data-selection signal can exist there, in either motion or
appearance. We confirm (ii) with two motion-free checks (source-sensitivity and
the encoder-gain test $\Delta_{\text{enc}}$). This set was fixed before any motion
distance was computed, so it is not an outcome-based exclusion.

\noindent\textbf{Appearance gains no predictive power from scratch.} The premise
behind training the matchers from scratch is that a randomly-initialized backbone,
lacking pretrained semantic features, might force the model to lean on raw
appearance, making an appearance distance newly predictive of which
target a source suits. This does not happen. In DINO appearance space the from-scratch
configurations are as null-to-wrong-signed as the pretrained ones
(Table~\ref{tab:lawdino_full}), and motion coverage remains the only positive
signal wherever the encoder learns at all. Removing pretrained features removes
predictive power without surfacing any in appearance.

\noindent\textbf{Source-sensitivity exposes the collapse.} Holding the target
image fixed, we swap the source image and record how far the predicted flow field
moves relative to its own magnitude, its \emph{source-sensitivity}. A genuine
matcher must move a great deal (a different source implies a different
correspondence); a model collapsed to a fixed field barely moves.
Table~\ref{tab:staticprior} reports it for the from-scratch backbone-dependent
matchers, averaged by target type and training-source type. The working matchers
on real motion (GLU-Net/FlowFormer) set the healthy scale at $5$--$9$; on semantic
targets every model falls to $\le0.5$, and under the semantic source to
${\approx}0.1$, no longer tracking the input.

\noindent\textbf{Encoder gain $\Delta_{\text{enc}}$.} The second check asks
whether training a from-scratch encoder beats \emph{leaving it random}.
Table~\ref{tab:degenerate} reports each from-scratch model's peak \pck{} with the

holds for every architecture on semantic targets and, for CATs++, even on
real motion (training the encoder \emph{costs} $8$ \pck). Because the criterion
never looks at a motion distance, it corroborates the cut without circularity, and
a higher-learning-rate schedule only collapses CATs++ further (not undertraining).

\section{Per-Configuration Evidence and the Pooled Flip}
\label{supp:law}

An earlier version of this analysis reported a pooled ``flip'' statistic
(measuring which single direction predicts better, averaged over configurations). We document it
here, and show it is an artifact of pooling real-motion and semantic benchmarks
(main Sec.~\ref{sec:law}): once the from-scratch$\times$semantic regime is separated
out, there is no flip to explain. We give the per-configuration evidence
together with the flip statistic and its (genuinely tight) robustness, so the
artifact is documented rather than hidden. The takeaway matches the main text.
Coverage is the consistent predictor under a backbone, the symmetric metrics
hedge, and the pooled flip is real as a pooled statistic but does not constitute a
separate rule.

\subsection{Per-configuration coverage vs.\ symmetric, all metrics}
Table~\ref{tab:supp_asym} reports, per configuration, the regime-matched
coverage correlation against both fixed directions and three symmetric baselines
(the symmetric mean-NN average, FID, and sliced Wasserstein-2, all in the
same BFV motion space). We also evaluated MMD but exclude it throughout, as its
kernel estimate was unstable across bandwidths and sampling budgets. Coverage's margin over the symmetric metrics is largest
where the two directions disagree most (pretrained GLU-Net), and the
symmetric metrics hedge everywhere, consistent with the main-paper claim that
direction-blind distances average the two failure modes together and so dilute
coverage under a backbone.

\subsection{Estimator replication: $\epsilon$-radius coverage}
Table~\ref{tab:supp_eps} replicates the coverage signal with a Monte-Carlo
$\epsilon$-radius support-overlap estimator at 1, 4, and 16\,px (thresholds
mapped into the normalized BFV space), in both the directed and the symmetric
framing. The directional structure is identical to mean-NN (coverage is the
predictive direction, off-target near-dead, symmetric in between), so the
asymmetry is not an artifact of the mean-NN estimator. On the in-design set,
$\epsilon$-4\,px coverage slightly \emph{edges} mean-NN on real-motion targets
(within-context $\rho$ $+0.64$ vs.\ $+0.60$) but trails it on semantic ($+0.36$
vs.\ $+0.53$) and overall ($+0.53$ vs.\ $+0.57$); 1\,px saturates (too strict) and
16\,px loosens. We keep the single mean-NN distance as the default. It is the best overall,
with no radius to tune. \emph{Learning a combination does not
help.} A linear combiner over all six directional $\epsilon$ features (three radii
$\times$ two directions), fit leave-one-source-out, overfits: within-context
$\rho{=}0.56$ in-fold but only $0.34$ held out, \emph{below} the single fit-free
distance ($0.57$), and even adding mean-NN to the six reaches just $0.45$. This is
a further instance of the learned-combiner negative control (main
Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}), where the extra granularity fits noise rather than signal.

\subsection{Where directionality does \emph{not} live: the anchor}
Directional borrowing of the level anchor $L$ (interpolating observed cells
with regime-matched directed similarities) \emph{loses} to plain symmetric
inverse-distance weighting on both anchor-interpolation problems
(held-out-benchmark MAE 16.9 symmetric vs.\ 18.9--24.1 directional;
held-out-source 8.6 vs.\ 8.7--10.1). Directionality lives in the ranking term
$g$, where learning happens, and not in the interpolation of the level anchor.

\subsection{Pretrained arm of the generated grid}
The pretrained (frozen-backbone) arm of the intervention grid spans nine
sources, but the grid was constructed to vary off-target mass, so the
missing-support distance is near-constant across these sources and any ranking
it induces is estimator-noise-limited. Its unstable point estimates nominally
favor the off-target direction ($\dtb$ $+0.64$, $\dbt$ $+0.21$, sym $+0.37$),
the \emph{opposite} of the pretrained prediction, which we report
undiscounted rather than omit, and we do not treat this arm as a test of the
pretrained half. A coverage-varying pretrained grid (varying $\dbt$ at fixed
$\dtb$) is the natural follow-up experiment.

produced by a moving camera or by correspondingly moving objects: a lateral
camera pan and a lateral object slide yield near-identical BFV histograms. This

\emph{aggregate} motion (the quantity that drives transfer) faithfully, and is
knowably agnostic to the camera/object factorization that produces it. A
target whose motion is dominated by camera/object ambiguity (e.g.\ a pure
lateral pan) is recovered as the corresponding object motion: the same
aggregate field by a different mechanism. Disentangling the two would require a
descriptor that encodes scene geometry, which is beyond the scope of this
motion-only study.

\section{Sampling Stability of the Estimators}
\label{supp:sampling}

We recompute every feature at flow-vector budgets from $5{\times}10^4$ to
$8{\times}10^6$ and correlate each budget's value against the largest
budget's, across all 110 source$\to$target pairs (Table~\ref{tab:sampling}).
Mean-NN distances in both
directions reach $\rho \ge 0.96$ by $5{\times}10^4$ vectors and $1.000$ at
the reference budget, and $\epsilon$-coverage at 4\,px behaves similarly. The

\section{The Middlebury Exclusion}
\label{supp:mid}

During analysis we observed that for pretrained variants the Middlebury
evaluation returned $52.6 \pm 4.7$ \pck{} regardless of training source
(within-context spread 4.7 vs.\ 10--17 on other benchmarks), while
from-scratch variants spread normally (15.3). Inspection identified a defect
in our Middlebury evaluation harness; pending re-evaluation, all reported
numbers exclude Middlebury (the remaining 9 benchmarks; 99 models, 891
evaluations). Sensitivity: with Middlebury included, every headline conclusion
is unchanged. The flip statistic is $+0.82$ rather than $+0.90$, and the
policy's held-out correlations shift by at most ${\sim}0.03$; the largest
single effect of the exclusion is on the pretrained-regime oracle fraction
($0.554\!\to\!0.612$), because
near-invariant contexts are unrankable and dilute within-context averages.

\subsection{Static-prior diagnostics, per training source}

\subsection{Off-target mass from scratch tracks encoder-degeneracy}

The encoder-degeneracy criterion itself (whether a trained-from-scratch encoder
beats a frozen random one, $\Delta_{\text{enc}}$) is reported in the main paper,
where it underwrites every exclusion. Here we add a complementary observation.
From scratch, off-target mass ($\dtb$) carries a weak secondary signal, and it
concentrates in the one architecture whose scratch encoder fails to learn. The table below gives, per
architecture, the within-context off-target and coverage correlations on
from-scratch real-motion cells, alongside how much training the scratch encoder
gains over a frozen random one (trained $-$ frozen):
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Arch. & off-target $\rho$ & coverage $\rho$ & encoder gain (trained $-$ frozen) \\
\midrule
CATs++ & $+0.65$ & $+0.11$ & $+1.3$ (\(\approx\)random) \\
GLU-Net & $+0.19$ & $+0.64$ & $+4.6$ \\

The relationship is monotone. The more the scratch encoder learns, the
less off-target mass matters and the more coverage dominates. Off-target mass
``working'' from scratch is therefore the same bias signature as the
scratch-semantic floor (it appears where the model leans on a prediction prior
rather than learned features), not a second selection rule. Pooled over scratch
real-motion cells it falls from $+0.33$ (all architectures) to $+0.18$ once the
encoder-degenerate CATs++ is removed, while coverage rises from $+0.42$ to
$+0.57$.

\subsection{Density is not the from-scratch--semantic signal (negative control)}
A natural alternative to the floor explanation is that from-scratch--semantic
transfer is helped by denser or more spatially-aligned supervision rather than
by motion. We tested this with source-side richness features (flow entropy,
moving-pixel fraction, log point count), a statistical partial-correlation
control, and a physical magnitude-equalization of the coverage distance. The
motion-richness features are \emph{negative} on from-scratch--semantic
($-0.38$/$-0.29$) and equally negative on from-scratch--real-motion; they track
unnatural, extreme motion (the high-entropy sources are the 2D-warp and
random-flipping variants), not a semantic-specific benefit. Spatial alignment is
a saturated non-signal for dense sources. And motion coverage on
from-scratch--semantic stays at the floor under both controls (raw $-0.20$,
statistical-partial $+0.14$, magnitude-equalized $-0.18$). Nothing source-side
ranks sources there. (Caveat: in our source set
label density is entangled with motion naturalness, so a clean causal test is a
label-density \emph{training} ablation, which we have not run.)

\subsection{Spatial vs.\ flow: decomposing the coverage distance}
BFV is four-dimensional ($x,y,\Delta x,\Delta y$), and the normalization gives
the spatial coordinates a wider range than the displacements, so the joint
nearest-neighbour distance is partly spatial. We recompute the two directed
distances in three sub-spaces of the same distribution (full, spatial-only ($x,y$),
and flow-only ($\Delta x,\Delta y$)) and re-run the stratified within-context
correlations. The clean axis is flow-coverage. It is positive wherever the
network can exploit motion (real-motion in both regimes, $+0.18$ scratch /
$+0.26$ pretrained; semantic only under a backbone, $+0.53$) and negative for
from-scratch--semantic ($-0.20$). The semantic precision signal is not a pure
spatial artifact (spatial-only off-target $\approx0$), and the spatial component
of coverage helps only in the from-scratch--semantic floor cell ($+0.43$),
exactly where flow-coverage is anti-predictive. So real-motion transfer is
predicted by motion coverage; from-scratch--semantic transfer, where it moves at
all, is predicted by spatial sampling rather than motion, reinforcing that the
floor is a place where motion is unusable.

\section{SDF-Fractals3D: Procedural Generation Details}
\label{supp:sdf}

SDF-Fractals3D is the one training source we generate ourselves; every other source
in the study is a public correspondence dataset. It is asset-free and renders paired
views on the fly from implicit signed-distance geometry, so a seed regenerates a
scene exactly and the parameter space spans a large range of distinct scenes with no
mesh or texture library. Generation factors into two independent stages: a motion
stage that samples the two camera viewpoints (with a controllable zoom distribution),
and an appearance stage that shades the rendered geometry buffers under independently
sampled lighting, texture, and background. Because the stages are independent, we can
intervene on motion while holding appearance fixed, which is what the interventions
in the main paper (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}) rely on. Fig.~\ref{fig:sdf_families} shows
the four shape families and two composed scenes.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/synth/shader_comparison_grid.png}
\caption{\textbf{SDF-Fractals3D shape families and composed scenes.} The four
signed-distance families (FBM terrain, Julia3D, Mandelbulb, radial displacement),
then two multi-object compositions, each rendered as a source/target view pair. The
generator remaps texture and lighting between the two views independently of geometry
(e.g.\ the Mandelbulb recolors purple to orange), so appearance varies while the
underlying 3D correspondence is unchanged.}
\label{fig:sdf_families}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Procedural shape families and latent sampling}
We build scenes from four families of signed-distance geometry, all evaluated on the
GPU at render time:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Mandelbulb.} A 3D escape-time fractal parameterized by a power $n$,
using a spherical-coordinate power map with iterative update
$\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \mathcal{P}_n(\mathbf{v}_t) + \mathbf{c}$, where
$\mathcal{P}_n(\cdot)$ is the $n$-power transform in spherical coordinates and
$\mathbf{c}$ is the latent fractal parameter.
\item \textbf{FBM terrain.} A height field on a plane built from multi-frequency
noise,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FBM}(\mathbf{p}) = \tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{i=0}^{3} A_i \big[
N(\mathbf{p}F_i) + N((\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{o}_1)F_i) + N((\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{o}_2)F_i)
\big] + 0.02 - k(p_x^2 + p_y^2),
\end{equation}
where $N(\cdot)$ is a procedural noise function, $A_i$ and $F_i$ are octave
amplitudes and frequencies, and the quadratic term bounds the surface spatially.
\item \textbf{Radial displacement.} A sphere-like base surface deformed by
procedural noise with radial displacement $h = \alpha\cdot\mathrm{noise} + \beta$,
for sampled scale $\alpha$ and offset $\beta$.
\item \textbf{Julia3D.} A 3D slice of a quaternion Julia set, defined by
$\mathbf{z}_{t+1} = \mathbf{z}_t^2 + \mathbf{c}$ with
$\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^4$, evaluated on a 3D cross-section for rendering.
\end{itemize}
Each sample draws its latent parameter $\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^4$ from a precomputed
grid by curvature-aware sampling. Writing $\kappa(\mathbf{c})$ for the stored
curvature score of a grid cell, we first pick a curvature interval $[\ell,\ell+s]$
and then sample uniformly over the cells whose scores fall inside it,
$p(\mathbf{c}) \propto \mathbf{1}[\kappa(\mathbf{c})\in[\ell,\ell+s]]$. The score
itself is $\kappa = 2\tfrac{\mu\sigma}{\mu+\sigma}(1+r)$, for precomputed curvature
statistics $\mu,\sigma$ and an edge-to-interior surface ratio $r$. This biases
sampling toward parameters that produce geometrically richer surfaces.

\subsection{View sampling and appearance}
The two camera views of a training pair are sampled jointly around a shared pair
center $(c_x,c_y)$,
\begin{equation}
(\theta_x^{(1)},\theta_y^{(1)}) = (c_x - v_x/2,\; c_y - v_y/2), \qquad
(\theta_x^{(2)},\theta_y^{(2)}) = (c_x + v_x/2,\; c_y + v_y/2),
\end{equation}
with the center chosen so both views stay inside fixed angular bounds. In the default
configuration $v_x, v_y \sim \mathcal{U}(-0.16667, 0.16666)$ in units of $\pi$, with
pair-center bounds $(0.5, 0.25)$. Normalized angles map to a camera position at fixed
radius $d=5$ through $\phi_x = \pi\theta_x$, $\phi_y = -\pi\theta_y$ and
$\mathbf{cam} = d\,[\sin\phi_x\cos\phi_y,\; -\sin\phi_y,\; \cos\phi_x\cos\phi_y]^\top$.
Scale is sampled jointly across the pair, $a\sim\mathcal{U}(0.5,0.9)$ and
$r\sim\mathcal{U}(-0.25,0)$, giving per-view scales $s^{(1)}=a$, $s^{(2)}=a+r$ and a
shader zoom $z^{(i)} = s^{(i)}/0.36$. The zoom distribution is the motion knob the
interventions steer. Appearance is sampled independently per view. Illumination uses
a single point light whose azimuth is drawn from a Gaussian centered near the viewing
direction, with ambient, diffuse, and specular strengths drawn from $[0.1,0.4]$,
$[0.2,0.4]$, and $[0.7,0.99]$ and a specular exponent from $\{32,\dots,74\}$.

\subsection{Scene composition and rendering}
Each sample holds a small collection of procedural SDF objects, fractal and
non-fractal, each with its own sampled latent code and spatial offset, and the
renderer produces two views of that single 3D scene with GLSL shaders. For each view
it outputs a dense 3D coordinate map $\mathbf{X}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}$
alongside surface normals and object identities, and procedural shading is applied
afterward to produce the RGB image. The separation matters: geometric correspondence
comes from the rendered 3D surface coordinates, while image appearance is decided
later by the shading stage, so the two are controllable on their own.

\subsection{Dense correspondence extraction}
Flow supervision comes from the rendered 3D surface coordinates rather than a
projected camera transform. For each valid target pixel $u_2$ we find the nearest
source pixel in 3D with FAISS,
\begin{equation}
u_1^\star = \arg\min_{u_1}\big\|\mathbf{X}^{(1)}(u_1) - \mathbf{X}^{(2)}(u_2)\big\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
and accept the match only when the 3D residual is small,
$\|\mathbf{X}^{(1)}(u_1^\star)-\mathbf{X}^{(2)}(u_2)\|_2^2 \le 5\times10^{-5}$. The
stored flow follows the target-to-source convention $\mathbf{f}(u_2) = u_1^\star - u_2$,
and pixels with no valid correspondence (occluded regions and background) are marked
invalid with flow $\infty$. So the base dataset carries true multi-view 3D motion,
view-dependent appearance change from re-rendering, and occlusion/disocclusion from
viewpoint change.

\subsection{Variant family}
The synthetic variants in the study share this generator and correspondence pipeline
and differ only through lightweight overrides, so they are controlled modifications of
one base pipeline rather than separate generators.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SDF-Fractals3D} is the base pipeline: paired view sampling, procedural
shading, and dense correspondence from two independently rendered views.
\item \textbf{SDF-Fractals3D (2D warp)} keeps the same sample and the same
target-to-source flow, but replaces the independently rendered target with a bilinear
inverse warp of the source, $I_t^{2D}(u) = I_s(u + \mathbf{f}(u))$, zero-padding where
the flow is invalid. This holds the displacement field exact while removing true
view-dependent appearance change and occlusion (Fig.~\ref{fig:sdf_pipeline}).
\item \textbf{SDF-Fractals3D (small zoom)} pins camera-angle variation near zero and
narrows the zoom range, cutting viewpoint change while keeping the pipeline.
\item \textbf{SDF-Fractals3D (large zoom)} also pins camera angle near zero but widens
the zoom range, emphasizing scale change over viewpoint change.
\item \textbf{SDF-Fractals3D (random flipping)} keeps the base generator and adds
random horizontal flips as augmentation.
\end{itemize}
