\section{Motion Coverage Predicts Transfer}
\label{sec:law}

\noindent\textbf{Transfer Study.}
We train four architectures, each in two \emph{configurations} with the backbone
frozen or fine-tuned (RAFT has no pre-trained backbone and trains end-to-end), on 11 training
sources and evaluate transfer to 9 target benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:study}). Six target benchmarks are real images:
KITTI-2012/2015, SPair-71K, PF-Pascal, PF-Willow, and TSS. Three are
synthetic: FlyingThings, PointOdyssey, and a held-out split of our SDF-Fractals3D. 

We measure transfer as peak \pck{} at $\alpha{=}5\%$, the fraction of predicted
correspondences within $5\%$ of image size of the true match. 
Coverage depends only on the (source, target) pair, while realized transfer also
depends on the model, so we evaluate every ranking within a fixed (target,
architecture, configuration) \emph{context}. We build each dataset's distributions from up to 8000 sampled scenes. For motion we
take 2000 flow vectors per scene, up to 16M in total, subsampling the dense datasets
to this budget while the sparse targets (SPair, PF-Pascal, PF-Willow, TSS) fall under
it and keep every vector. For appearance we take a $32\times32$ patch grid per scene,
up to 8M appearance vectors.

We score a distance's predicted source ordering against the realized one with the
Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ ($+1$ identical order, $0$ unrelated, $-1$ reversed),
computed within each context so benchmark and model effects cannot inflate it. For
absolute prediction we report the Pearson correlation $r$ between predicted and actual
\pck{}, with the mean absolute error in \pck{} points. For selection we report the
median top-1 regret, measured within each context as the \pck{} that the distance's
top-ranked source gives up against the true best source, and reported as the median
of that gap across contexts. Throughout we stratify targets into \emph{real-motion} and
\emph{semantic} sets (Table~\ref{tab:study}).

We evaluate both motion and appearance representations on this transfer study,
scoring every (source, target) pair with each distance (Tables~\ref{tab:lawdino}
and~\ref{tab:law}). In DINOv3 feature space, every distance is near zero or negative (Table~\ref{tab:lawdino}). Negating these distances
does not recover a usable predictor; the weak negative trend means only that visually
dissimilar sources transfer marginally better, an effect too incidental to guide
selection. Appearance offers no consistent, actionable signal for which source best suits a given target.

However, motion presents a consistent, actionable signal. A source whose motion covers
the target's tends to transfer better, and the pattern holds across every architecture and
encoder state (Table~\ref{tab:law}, Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage_law_a}), with coverage
($\dbt$) being either the best predictor or positively correlated in every cell. This implies the signal is directional, and concentrated in
the coverage term of the Chamfer distance, while the off-target half ($\dtb$) stays near zero. Symmetric
distances (Chamfer, sliced $W_2$) mix these two directions which generally results in a weaker signal.
Notably, most motion distances correlate positively with transfer, and coverage most consistent of all. This holds even on semantic keypoint targets, where a source's motion
statistics have no obvious reason to predict matching, though in the semantic cases the symmetric
distances (Chamfer, $W_2$) become competitive with coverage.

We do not test the mechanism directly, but the pattern suggests a simple one. A model
trained on a source whose motion covers the target's has seen the target's
displacement patterns during training, so its matching head is exposed to the
correspondences the target demands, and motion coverage measures that overlap. The same
logic extends to the semantic targets, whose keypoint correspondences are also
displacements, so a source that spans them trains a matcher that handles them, even
without photorealism or semantic content in the source. Concretely, our asset-free SDF-Fractals3D shares no objects or textures with any semantic target, yet outranks the real-image training sources on them (full transfer grid in the supplementary material, Table~\ref{S-tab:transfergrid}). 

\noindent\textbf{Robustness.} To ensure the effect does not hinge on any one source, model, or
estimator we perform a series of robustness checks. We find the effect holds for all four architectures and both encoder states
(Table~\ref{tab:law}) and across two alternative distance estimators (epsilon radius, and k-means clustering, see supplementary Sect.~\ref{S-supp:law}). 

We also perform jackknife ablations by dropping each
training source individually, and then entire training source families (Kubric, realflow, SDF-Fractals3D, semantic, and 2D-warps). We find the
spearman correlation remains within $[{+}0.43,{+}0.54]$, so no single source or family is load-bearing (see supplementary Sect.~\ref{S-supp:robust}).

We also test whether the BFV is a proxy for dataset size or the density of supervised
flow vectors, by ranking sources by each. Dataset size ranks sources at
$\rho{\approx}0.04$ and flow-vector density at $\rho{\le}0.27$, both well below motion
coverage (see supplementary Sect.~\ref{S-supp:robust}).

We further test sampling sensitivity by ablating through different sample budgets (50K, 100K, 500K, 1M, 8M, 16M). The directed distances converge by 50K samples and stay stable through our budget range. Whereas the density-based KNN-KL divergence never converges.
Per-configuration tables, the two estimators, and the sampling and pooling controls
are in the supplement (Sect.~\ref{S-supp:law} and~\ref{S-supp:sampling}).
