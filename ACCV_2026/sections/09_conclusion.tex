\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

What predicts synthetic-to-real correspondence transfer is motion
\emph{coverage}: how well a source covers the target's motion. That a source
should move like its target is not a new idea; what we add is that the useful
form of it is directional, and cheap to check. Ranking candidate sources by one
directed motion distance already sits near the reproducibility ceiling of transfer
($92\%$ of it).
As a single default it matches or leads every symmetric and distribution distance,
which dilute the signal with near-useless off-target mass; where a symmetric distance
is competitive (some semantic targets, and end-to-end RAFT), it is the recall it shares
with coverage, not its off-target term, that carries the signal; and it has no
appearance-space analogue: how the frames look does not tell you which target a
source suits. None of this needs
training a network or rendering a frame.

Because off-target motion is nearly free, coverage does two jobs. To
\emph{select} among existing sources, rank them by coverage. To \emph{design} new
data, add coverage of the target instead of matching its distribution, the move
our render-free search exploits on a real target. The same rule guided two very
different generators, a 3D scene simulator and a procedural implicit-surface
generator, so it is a property of the data, not of any one renderer. The recipe
is short: before you improve how you render, measure how well your source covers
the target's motion.
