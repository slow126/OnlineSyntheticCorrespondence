\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Visual correspondence---dense optical flow, dense geometric matching, and sparse
semantic (keypoint) matching---relies heavily on synthetic training data, because
ground-truth correspondence is scarce on real images
\cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset,sintel,greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}. Most
progress targets \emph{how} to render this data (fidelity, lighting, speed)
\cite{greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}, on the assumption that closing the
appearance gap---photorealism, and scenes filled with realistic, target-like
objects---drives transfer. Yet non-photorealistic data often transfers well
\cite{Baradad2021LookingAtNoise,white2009mandelbulb}, and a logically prior
question gets far less attention: given a controllable pipeline, what
\emph{motion} should its scenes contain for a given target---not which objects
appear, but how they move? As rendering becomes more efficient and the
appearance gap narrows, the motion a source generates becomes the binding constraint.

Across our study, appearance carries little \emph{target-selective} signal. In a
strong self-supervised feature space---DINOv3~\cite{simeoni2025dinov3}, which
encodes object identity, texture, and semantic category---appearance similarity
does not predict which source suits which target, and the most realistic renders
(photorealistic lighting with real scanned objects) give no transfer edge over
flat-shaded procedural shapes. Appearance sets a source's overall quality, not
which target it serves.

By contrast, motion predicts transfer: how well a source's motion matches the
target's tracks how well it transfers. We measure that match with standard
distribution distances---sliced Wasserstein, FID~\cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate},
and the (symmetric) Chamfer distance---but the sharpest signal comes from
\emph{decomposing} the Chamfer into its two directed halves and keeping only one.
That half is \textbf{coverage}---how well the source's motion covers the target's
(a recall-analogue)---and it is the consistent predictor of transfer across
architectures, benchmarks, and estimators (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}). The other half,
\textbf{off-target mass} (motion the source renders but the target never uses, a
precision-analogue), carries little signal and only dilutes it---which is why all
three symmetric distances, each of which folds it back in, track transfer more
weakly than coverage alone.

Our evidence comes from 76 trained correspondence models: four architecture
families---the semantic matcher CATs++ \cite{cho2022cats++}, the dense matcher
GLU-Net \cite{truong2020glunet}, and the optical-flow networks RAFT
\cite{teed2020raft} and FlowFormer \cite{huang2022flowformer}---in
seven backbone configurations, trained over 11 real and synthetic sources and
evaluated on 9 real and synthetic benchmarks ($684$ transfer measurements). We
stress-test the coverage finding
from several angles: replication across three distance estimators, a negative
control in appearance-feature space, a geometry-only construct-validity check in
which matching the motion descriptor alone recovers each target's expected motion
regime, and a controllable grid crossing motion against appearance on which a
target-tuned source beats a generic baseline.

We make four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A broad transfer study} (Sect.~\ref{sec:setup}): correspondence
  transfer between real and synthetic datasets, across four architecture families in
  seven backbone configurations, over 11 training sources and 9 benchmarks spanning
  dense, geometric, and semantic matching ($684$ transfer measurements).
\item \textbf{Motion coverage predicts transfer} (Sects.~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:absolute}):
  how well a source's motion \emph{covers} the target's---one direction of a Chamfer
  distance between their flow distributions---is the most reliable zero-shot
  predictor of transfer we find, ranking sources near the ceiling set by transfer's
  own reproducibility. Appearance similarity carries little target-selective signal,
  and because rendering off-target motion is nearly free, symmetric distances that
  average it back in only dilute the signal.
\item \textbf{A lightweight, renderer-agnostic way to tune a source}
  (Sect.~\ref{sec:interv}): because motion coverage can be scored from a
  geometry-only pass (the scene's motion, with no textures or lighting) without
  training a network, a cheap derivative-free search over generator parameters
  aligns a controllable source's motion to a target. We do not claim a global
  optimum---only that the coverage objective is informative enough for local search
  to reach a good candidate---and the same objective steers two structurally
  different generators.
\item \textbf{Tuning improves downstream transfer} (Sect.~\ref{sec:interv}): the
  motion-tuned source reliably beats a generic Kubric baseline across architectures
  and benchmarks, on both a camera-motion target (KITTI) and a semantic one (TSS),
  with no real target labels.
\end{enumerate}
