We describe each dataset's motion and appearance as a point cloud and measure directed distances between them; the direction matters because a coverage gap and excess off-target mass are distinct failure modes that a symmetric measure averages away (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}).

\noindent\textbf{Motion representation.}
We summarize each dataset as a point cloud of flow vectors, each recording a
pixel's image-space location alongside its optical flow displacement:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{flow}} = \bigl[\hat{x},\;\hat{y},\;\Delta\hat{x},\;\Delta\hat{y}\bigr],
\label{eq:bfv}
\end{equation}
where pixel coordinates are normalized to $[-1,1]$ by image dimensions
($\hat{x} = 2x/W - 1$, $\hat{y} = 2y/H - 1$), and flow displacements are
expressed in the same spatial units ($\Delta\hat{x} = 2\,\Delta x/W$,
$\Delta\hat{y} = 2\,\Delta y/H$), so a displacement of $\pm 1$ corresponds to
half the image width or height.
This bag-of-flow-vectors (BFV) representation (Fig.~\ref{fig:splats}) retains
\emph{where} motion occurs and \emph{how} content moves while remaining invariant
to image resolution and free of any spatial binning; the joint encoding is
intentional---spatially-grounded motion patterns (e.g., a radial expansion field
for ego-motion, vertical scatter for object-centric scenes) carry information that
flow direction alone discards.
BFV is intentionally coarse: it collapses each frame pair into a bag of
pixel-level displacements without decomposing motion into its underlying physical
causes (parallax, depth discontinuities, occluder boundaries, surface velocity),
and it carries no appearance information.
This simplification is the design choice under test---we ask whether a crude
motion fingerprint, requiring no scene understanding and no photorealistic
rendering, is sufficient to predict where a source will transfer.

\noindent\textbf{Appearance representation.}
As an appearance control we represent each dataset using DINOv3 patch
features~\cite{simeoni2025dinov3}, reduced by PCA and $\ell_2$-normalized.
DINOv3 is trained by self-supervision on natural images with no exposure to any
of our correspondence tasks, so its feature space is not shaped by transfer
outcomes---though it naturally reflects the texture, illumination, and material
statistics of the images it was trained on rather than providing a neutral
decomposition of scene properties.
We apply the same distance framework to both descriptors so that
any difference in predictive power reflects the descriptor's content, not the
measurement procedure.
A reproducibility ceiling (Sect.~\ref{sec:predict}) contextualizes how much
systematic ranking signal is available at all: for each architecture family we
measure how often two same-family models trained under different backbone
configurations (frozen vs.\ fine-tuned) agree on which training source is better,
bounding what any data-only predictor could achieve on these benchmarks.
Motion coverage accounts for $\approx$78\% of this ceiling within our study; the
remaining gap reflects a combination of architectural sensitivity to the backbone
freeze choice and any factors our benchmarks do not cleanly separate---including
appearance effects that DINOv3 features aggregated over large natural-image
corpora may not resolve for synthetic training sets.

\noindent\textbf{Distribution distances.}
We evaluate several standard distribution distances---symmetric Chamfer,
FID~\cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate}, and sliced Wasserstein-2---and
find that decomposing the Chamfer into its two directed halves is the more
reliable choice on aggregate; on individual benchmarks, particularly semantic
targets, symmetric distances remain competitive and for FlowFormer on semantic
targets are clearly stronger (Sect.~\ref{sec:law}).
Writing $d_{A \to B}$ for the directed distance from a training cloud $A$ to a
target cloud $B$,
\begin{equation}
d_{A \to B} \;=\; \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a \in A}\; \min_{b \in B}\,
\lVert a - b \rVert_2^2,
\label{eq:dirdist}
\end{equation}
this is the one-sided Chamfer distance~\cite{fan2017pointcloud}.
Writing $\dtb$ for the train-to-target direction and $\dbt$ for the reverse,
$\dtb$ is large when much of the training distribution lies far from anything
the target uses (\textbf{off-target mass}), and $\dbt$ is large when target
motions have no nearby training example (\textbf{missing support}).
Whether a symmetric measure meaningfully conflates these two cases is an empirical
question; we find the directed split to be the more reliable predictor on balance
(Table~\ref{tab:predictors}).

We use support-geometry estimators rather than density-based divergences (e.g., KL): on procedurally generated clouds, rendering more frames changes estimated density without changing support geometry, so KL does not converge; support-geometry estimators are stable, and the alternatives we evaluate all agree on the central finding (supp., Sect.~\ref{sec:law}).

\noindent\textbf{Transfer study.}
We study four architecture families over 11 synthetic and real training sources,
evaluated on 9 benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:study}). Six of the nine targets are
real images (KITTI-2012/2015, SPair-71K, PF-Pascal, PF-Willow, TSS); FlyingThings,
PointOdyssey, and the procedural split are synthetic targets, included for their
controlled motion and exact labels. These have real 3D geometry and look markedly
different from one another and from the sources, so coverage-predictable
cross-transfer among them is itself a test of the motion-over-appearance thesis.
Among \emph{real} images, dense-motion transfer is carried mainly by KITTI---real
dense flow labels are scarce, which is the premise of this work.
The coverage score depends only on the (source, target) pair, but a source's
realized transfer also depends on the model, so we evaluate every ranking claim
within a fixed (benchmark, architecture, configuration) \emph{context}; this
same context indexes the level term of the absolute predictor
(Sect.~\ref{sec:absolute}). We report \emph{peak} \pck{} (the best checkpoint per
run) so data quality is not confounded with convergence speed: peak selection is a
constant, monotone treatment that leaves source rankings untouched while affecting
only the absolute level, which we predict separately. Throughout, coverage is
stratified into \emph{real-motion} and \emph{semantic} targets
(Table~\ref{tab:study}), as the two strata behave differently (Sect.~\ref{sec:law}).
We use Spearman $\rho$ for all \emph{ranking} claims and Pearson $r$ (with MAE)
for \emph{absolute-\pck} prediction (Sect.~\ref{sec:absolute}); the pooled scatter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage_law_a}) shows Pearson $r$ on within-context-standardized
values for visualization, but the load-bearing statistic throughout is Spearman
$\rho$.

\noindent\textbf{From-scratch training (out-of-distribution probe).}
We also trained each backbone matcher with randomly-initialized feature extractors
to probe whether appearance becomes a stronger signal when pretrained semantics are
absent.
The from-scratch encoders collapse to a near-static prior that ignores the image
pair entirely, so neither descriptor is a meaningful source-ranking predictor
there: a model insensitive to its input provides no data-selection signal for
either modality.
We treat this regime as out-of-distribution, report it in full in the supplement,
and note that the null result for appearance here is a consequence of input-blindness
rather than an independent test of the appearance hypothesis.
The configurations were fixed before any motion distance was measured.
