\begin{abstract}
Synthetic data is the default for training visual correspondence models, but
choosing which synthetic dataset will transfer best to a given target is still
largely guesswork. We show the choice can be made by measurement, before a
single frame is rendered. Representing each dataset by the distribution of its
motion---a crude bag of flow vectors---a training set can mismatch a target in
two directed ways: by placing mass on motions the target never uses
(\emph{off-target mass}, $\dtb$), or by failing to \emph{cover} the motions the
target needs (\emph{coverage}, $\dbt$). Across 1{,}278 transfer evaluations
(four architecture families---CATs++, RAFT, GLU-Net, FlowFormer---in 13 backbone
configurations $\times$ 11 synthetic training sets $\times$ 9 benchmarks; 142
trained models), \emph{motion coverage is what governs transfer}.

Coverage is the single best predictor we find: within-context $\rho{=}0.52$,
$88\%$ of the reproducibility ceiling of transfer itself. It beats every
symmetric and distribution distance---FID, sliced Wasserstein-2, MMD, and the
symmetric mean-NN average ($+0.39$ to $+0.42$)---because each folds in off-target
mass, which on its own barely predicts ($+0.13$); and it far beats the identical
measurement in appearance (DINO) feature space, which is null-to-wrong-signed.
The actionable form is counter-intuitive: \emph{cover} the target's motion,
do not \emph{match} its distribution---carrying extra off-target motion is
nearly free. The one place coverage fails is training \emph{from scratch on
semantic targets}, which do not learn the task at all (transfer at the floor; a
trained-from-scratch encoder no better than a random one)---an
out-of-distribution \emph{model} regime we identify and set aside.

The result is a fit-free, render-free selection rule: rank candidate sources by
motion coverage. It picks a training set within a median $0.6$ \pck{} of the
best available (versus $17$ at random) and orders large-gap candidates correctly
$77\%$ of the time, matching an independent retraining---at zero training cost.
Because the descriptor needs no differentiable renderer, it drives a closed-loop
search over a generator's motion parameters that realizes its predicted gain on
a real target ($+0.93$ predicted, $+0.87$ realized) with a null control. Cover
the motion, not the look.
\keywords{Dense correspondence \and Synthetic training data \and Dataset
selection \and Transferability}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Dense visual correspondence---optical flow, geometric and semantic
matching---relies on synthetic training data more than almost any other vision
task, because dense ground truth is scarce and expensive to obtain on real
images \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset,sintel,greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}.
Most recent progress targets \emph{how} to render this data---higher fidelity,
better lighting, faster and lighter pipelines
\cite{greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}---on the assumption that narrowing the
appearance gap to real images is what drives transfer, an assumption already
complicated by abstract, non-photorealistic data that transfers surprisingly
well \cite{Baradad2021LookingAtNoise,white2009mandelbulb}. A logically prior
question has received far less attention: given a controllable pipeline,
\emph{what} should it render for a particular target? Rendering is now cheap;
the binding constraint is deciding what to generate, and for whom.

A structural difficulty underlies this question: the value of a synthetic
dataset is not a property of the dataset alone---it depends on the target. The
source that transfers best to one target is middling for another, so ``best
dataset'' is meaningful only relative to where the model will be deployed. The
right comparison rests on a simple fact: matching a training distribution to a
target's is inherently \emph{directional}. In a shared motion descriptor space,
a training set can mismatch a target in two distinct ways---it can place mass on
motions the target never uses (\textbf{off-target mass}; precision-analogous),
or fail to cover the motions the target requires (\textbf{coverage}, or missing
support; recall-analogous)---which we measure as the two directed
nearest-neighbor distances between the datasets' flow-vector distributions. Symmetric discrepancies---FID
\cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate}, MMD
\cite{gretton2008kernelmethodtwosampleproblem}, sliced Wasserstein---average
these two failure modes together. Generative-model evaluation separates them to
assess sample quality \cite{sajjadi2018assessing_precision_recall,kynkaanniemi2019improved_pr};
we show that for predicting \emph{transfer} the separation is not merely
diagnostic but decisive---one direction carries almost all the signal:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The coverage principle.} \emph{Motion coverage---how well a source's
motion distribution covers the target's, measured by the directed distance
$\dbt$---is what governs correspondence transfer. It is the single best
predictor of which synthetic source transfers best, across architectures and
training regimes; off-target mass (the reverse direction) barely matters, so one
should \emph{cover} the target's motion rather than \emph{match} its
distribution. The only models this fails for are those that do not learn the
task at all.}
Two consequences run through the paper. First, because off-target mass carries
little signal, the symmetric and distribution distances that charge it (FID,
Wasserstein, MMD, the symmetric average) all trail coverage---adding it back is
dead weight, not denoising. Second, the appearance analogue of coverage is null:
what predicts transfer is motion structure, not how the frames look. Throughout,
``pretrained'' refers to the \emph{image-feature backbone} (\eg, ImageNet
ResNet-101), not to correspondence-task pre-training; the correspondence head is
always trained on the synthetic source.
\end{quote}

We establish this on a transfer study of 142 trained correspondence
models---four architecture families (CATs++ \cite{cho2022cats++}, RAFT
\cite{teed2020raft}, GLU-Net \cite{truong2020glunet}, and FlowFormer
\cite{huang2022flowformer}) in 13 backbone configurations $\times$ 11 training
sources, evaluated on 9 benchmarks (1{,}278 transfer measurements)---and test it
four independent ways: replication across three distance estimators, a negative
control in appearance-feature space, a closed-loop intervention in which the
coverage signal steers a generator and training confirms the predicted gain,
and a construct-validity check in which matching the descriptor alone recovers
each target's physically correct motion. (A direct \emph{causal} test of
coverage---a grid that varies coverage at fixed off-target mass---we flag as the
main missing experiment.)

We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The coverage principle} (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}): motion coverage
  ($\dbt$) is the single best predictor of correspondence transfer, on every
  architecture and training configuration that learns the task---within-context
  $\rho{=}0.52$, $88\%$ of the reproducibility ceiling. It beats every symmetric
  and distribution distance, and the effect is motion-specific: the identical
  analysis in DINO appearance space carries no signal. The lone exception is
  training from scratch on semantic targets, which do not learn the task at all;
  we identify this as an out-of-distribution \emph{model} regime and set it aside
  (a regime ``flip'' we initially reported is an artifact of pooling it in).
\item \textbf{A fit-free, render-free selection rule at the reproducibility
  ceiling} (Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}): \emph{rank candidate sources by motion
  coverage $\dbt$}---no target-side training, no learned weights, no
  differentiable renderer. It picks a training set within a median $0.6$ \pck{}
  of the best available (vs.\ $17$ at random) and orders large-gap candidates
  correctly $77\%$ of the time, matching an independent retraining; the identical
  analysis on DINO appearance features carries no signal.
\item \textbf{Cover, don't match}
  (Secs.~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:predict}): off-target mass barely predicts
  transfer ($\rho{=}0.13$), so the symmetric and distribution distances (FID,
  sliced Wasserstein-2, MMD) trail coverage by folding it in. The actionable
  consequence is that carrying motion the target does not need is nearly
  free---generator design should \emph{add} coverage, not match the
  distribution. This also explains why direction-blind distribution
  matching---including our own earlier results---washed out in this space.
\item \textbf{Ranking zero-shot, calibration only with anchors}
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:absolute}): coverage ranks candidates without any target-side
  training, but absolute \pck{} is not recoverable from motion statistics
  alone---it is set largely by a source-level appearance/realism effect the
  motion distances do not see. Given a few observed runs as anchors, the
  coverage score interpolates absolute \pck{} (MAE ${\approx}9{-}10$ held-out;
  $r{=}0.90$ under a backbone); absent anchors we claim ranking only.
\item \textbf{Interventional support} (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}): a render-free
  generator search, scored only by motion coverage, improves a real-data target
  as predicted ($+0.93{\to}+0.87$ \pck{}) with a null control; matching the
  descriptor alone recovers each target's physically correct motion (construct
  validity); and appearance-stripped twins with bit-identical motion isolate
  motion---not appearance---as the carrier of transfer.
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit about scope. The coverage law holds on every regime where the
model learns the task and survives every robustness control we ran (estimator
replication, leave-one-benchmark-out, self-pair exclusion, generator-family
jackknife); the one excluded regime---from scratch on semantic targets---fails
to learn the task at all, and we treat it as out of distribution rather than a
counterexample (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). Coverage's predictive role is
established observationally; its \emph{causal} test---a grid that varies
coverage at fixed off-target mass---is future work, though the closed-loop
search is suggestive. Ranking at $\rho{\approx}0.5$ caps within-context
explainable variance at ${\sim}25\%$; absolute accuracy beyond that comes from
anchors, not features.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Synthetic data for correspondence.}
FlyingChairs/FlyingThings \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset}, Sintel
\cite{sintel}, Kubric/MOVi \cite{greff2021kubric}, and PointOdyssey
\cite{pointodyssey} established synthetic pre-training as the default for
dense matching; AutoFlow \cite{sun2021autoflow} and DFlow \cite{im2022dflow}
\emph{learn} data parameters with training in the loop. Closest to our
premise, Mayer~\etal~\cite{mayer2018what} showed that matching target
displacement statistics improves flow training. That result is undirected and
regime-blind; our central finding is precisely the part it could not see: the
match is \emph{directional}---coverage of the target's motion is the half that
governs transfer---and a pretrained backbone is what lets a model use it.
A large, active line of work improves the \emph{rendering} side of these
pipelines---photorealism, lighting, scale, and speed
\cite{greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}; this is complementary to, but distinct
from, our question. We take the renderer as given and instead supply a
target-conditioned signal for \emph{what} to generate, which is why the same
rule guides two structurally different generators (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}).
Procedural abstraction \cite{Baradad2021LookingAtNoise,white2009mandelbulb}
supports our use of non-photorealistic, motion-controllable generators.

\noindent\textbf{Transferability estimation.}
LEEP \cite{nguyen2020leep}, LogME \cite{you2021logme}, NCE
\cite{tran2019transferability}, H-score \cite{bao2019information}, and OTCE
\cite{tan2021otce} predict transfer from model outputs or target labels,
almost exclusively for classification. OTDD \cite{alvarez2020geometric} is,
like ours, a fit-free dataset distance, but symmetric and label-dependent.
None address dense correspondence, none are regime-conditional, and none are
directional; our experiments show the symmetric choice is not a detail but
the reason such signals wash out across regimes.

\noindent\textbf{Precision/recall divergences.}
Directional two-sample diagnostics are established in generative-model
evaluation \cite{sajjadi2018assessing_precision_recall,
kynkaanniemi2019improved_pr,naeem2020reliable,alaa2022faithful}. We adopt the
same decomposition in motion space and make it \emph{predictive and
regime-indexed} for transfer, which to our knowledge is new.

\noindent\textbf{Regime-conditional data value.}
Sorscher~\etal~\cite{sorscher2022beyond} showed the value of easy vs.\ hard
examples flips with data abundance; pretraining-data selection methods such
as DSIR \cite{xie2023data} and DoReMi \cite{xie2023doremi} match
distributions symmetrically. Our result is a new instance of
regime-conditional data value on a new axis (scratch vs.\ pretrained backbone;
directed motion coverage). On the theory side, asymmetric transfer exponents
\cite{hanneke2019value} and covariate-shift support conditions anticipate
exactly our headline---coverage of the target's support is what governs
transfer. What those arguments do not anticipate is how cheaply it can be
measured (a crude motion histogram, no training, no differentiable renderer) or
that off-target mass is nearly free, so coverage---not symmetric distribution
matching---is the objective. Our dataset-size controls (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}) rule
out the trivial ``more data'' explanation of the source ordering.

\noindent\textbf{Architectures and backbones.}
We span semantic matching (CATs++ \cite{cho2022cats++}), recurrent flow (RAFT
\cite{teed2020raft}), and pyramid dense matching (GLU-Net
\cite{truong2020glunet}); appearance descriptors use DINOv3
\cite{simeoni2025dinov3}, whose strong zero-shot matching
\cite{sd_dino_tale_of_two_features} motivates appearance as a serious
competing hypothesis---which our controls reject for within-context dataset
ranking.

\section{Setup: Two Directed Distances and a Decomposition}
\label{sec:setup}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/splats/composite_overview.png}
\caption{\textbf{Motion fingerprints (BFV splats).} Each panel aggregates a
dataset's joint flow vectors $\mathbf{f}=[\hat x,\hat y,\Delta\hat
x,\Delta\hat y]$, clusters them spatially (non-uniform, data-driven
clusters), and renders each cluster as a directional splat: color encodes
dominant flow direction (legend), splat extent encodes the cluster's spread.
The first three columns hold the same dataset in both splits (train above
eval); their fingerprints match, confirming the signature is a property of the
data rather than of a particular split. Training sets and benchmarks otherwise
occupy visibly different regions of motion space; the two directed distances
defined below quantify the two distinct ways their supports can fail to align.}
\label{fig:splats}
\end{figure}

We represent each dataset by the distribution of its motion and ask how well a
training set's distribution covers a target's. This section defines that
representation, the two directed distances that are our central measurement,
and the decomposition we use to turn them into predictions.

\noindent\textbf{Transfer study.}
We study four architecture families: the semantic matcher CATs++
\cite{cho2022cats++}, the optical-flow networks RAFT \cite{teed2020raft} and
FlowFormer \cite{huang2022flowformer}, and the dense-matching network GLU-Net
\cite{truong2020glunet}. For CATs++, GLU-Net, and FlowFormer the image-feature
backbone (ImageNet ResNet-101, VGG, and a pretrained Twins-SVT transformer,
respectively) is either initialized from those features or trained from
scratch, and either frozen or trained jointly with the correspondence head,
giving four configurations each; RAFT is trained from scratch by construction
and is one. The resulting \textbf{13 backbone configurations} are each trained
on 11 pure synthetic sources (\textbf{142 trained models}; one FlowFormer cell
did not complete) and evaluated by peak \pck@0.05 on 9 benchmarks spanning
optical flow, dense, and semantic matching (KITTI-2012/2015 \cite{kitti_flow},
FlyingThings \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset}, PointOdyssey
\cite{pointodyssey}, TSS, PF-Pascal/PF-Willow \cite{pfpascal}, SPair-71k
\cite{spair71k}, and a held-out split of our procedural source), for
\textbf{1{,}278 source--benchmark--configuration transfer measurements}. We
treat each (benchmark, architecture, configuration) triple as a \emph{context}
and make all ranking claims within contexts. Throughout we stratify the nine
benchmarks into \emph{real-motion} targets (KITTI-2012/2015, FlyingThings,
PointOdyssey, and the procedural split), whose ground truth is dense pixel
motion, and \emph{semantic} targets (SPair-71k, PF-Pascal, PF-Willow, TSS),
whose ground truth is sparse keypoint correspondence across object instances;
this distinction is load-bearing for the from-scratch regime.

\noindent\textbf{Motion representation.}
We summarize each dataset as a point cloud of flow vectors in a normalized
four-dimensional space of image position and displacement,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{flow}} = [\hat{x},\, \hat{y},\, \Delta\hat{x},\,
\Delta\hat{y}] \in [-1,1]^4,
\label{eq:bfv}
\end{equation}
following the bag-of-flow-vectors representation (Fig.~\ref{fig:splats}). This
retains \emph{where} motion occurs and \emph{how} content moves while remaining
invariant to image resolution and free of any spatial binning. For the
appearance control, we build the analogous representation from DINOv3 patch
features, reduced by PCA and $\ell_2$-normalized \cite{simeoni2025dinov3}.

\noindent\textbf{Two directed distances.}
The match between a training cloud $A$ and a target cloud $B$ is directional,
and we measure each direction by the mean nearest-neighbor distance
\begin{equation}
d_{A \to B} \;=\; \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a \in A}\; \min_{b \in B}\,
\lVert a - b \rVert_2^2 .
\label{eq:dirdist}
\end{equation}
Writing $\dtb$ for the train-to-target direction and $\dbt$ for the reverse,
$\dtb$ is large when much of the training distribution lies far from anything
the target uses (\textbf{off-target mass}), and $\dbt$ is large when target
motions have no nearby training example (\textbf{missing support}).

Each direction can be estimated in several ways, and the choice is not
incidental. Our default, Eq.~(\ref{eq:dirdist}), is a \emph{support-geometry}
estimator: it depends only on how close the two point sets lie, not on their
local densities. We contrast it with two alternatives---$\epsilon$-coverage,
the fraction of one cloud lying within a radius of the other (also
support-based), and a $k$-nearest-neighbor estimate of the KL divergence
(density-based). For procedurally generated data, the support-based estimators
are the principled choice. What governs transfer is \emph{which} motions a
dataset covers, not how often each one is rendered; yet rendering more frames
of the same motion inflates a density estimate while leaving the support
unchanged, so a density divergence such as KL entangles an intrinsic property
of the data with an artifact of the sampling budget. This is not hypothetical:
the nearest-neighbor and $\epsilon$-coverage estimates converge as the sample
grows, whereas the KL estimate fails to stabilize at any budget we tried
(supp.). Among the three, nearest-neighbor is the most informative predictor
and is our default, $\epsilon$-coverage is close behind, and KL is both the
least stable and the weakest; that all three nonetheless agree on the central
finding (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}) shows it reflects the \emph{direction} of the
comparison rather than the estimator. As direction-blind baselines we also use
the symmetric average of the two directions and three distribution
distances---FID \cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate}, sliced
Wasserstein-2, and MMD \cite{gretton2008kernelmethodtwosampleproblem}.

\noindent\textbf{Prediction decomposition.}
Absolute transfer accuracy varies far more across benchmarks and architectures
than across training sources, so we separate a context-level term from a
within-context ranking term,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{\pck}}(i,k,v) \;=\; \underbrace{L(k,v)}_{\text{level anchor}}
\;+\; \underbrace{g(i \to k)}_{\text{within-context ranking}},
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is interpolated from observed runs and $g$ ranks sources within a
context. Directionality enters only through $g$, where learning happens; the
anchor $L$ is best interpolated with a direction-blind similarity (supp.). We
report three leakage-safe protocols of increasing difficulty---holding out the
source, holding out the benchmark, and holding out both---so that for the
hardest protocol nothing about a predicted (source, benchmark) pair has been
seen during fitting.

Table~\ref{tab:law} and Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} present the core finding. For
every training configuration we correlate transfer with each directed distance
within contexts. One column is the consistent positive predictor: \emph{coverage}
($\dbt$, bold), how well the source's motion covers the target's. On
real-motion targets it is positive in every configuration and strong wherever
the encoder learns (up to $+0.73$); under a pretrained backbone it predicts on
semantic targets too (CATs++ $+0.50$, GLU-Net $+0.80$). Off-target mass ($\dtb$)
and the symmetric distances (sym, W2) are erratic by comparison---competitive in
some cells, wrong-signed in others. The same analysis in DINOv3 appearance space is
the negative control (Table~\ref{tab:lawdino}): no appearance distance is a
usable positive predictor in either stratum, and on semantic targets it is
consistently wrong-signed. Whatever Table~\ref{tab:law} captures lives in
motion, not in what the frames look like.

\noindent\textbf{Coverage governs wherever the model learns the task.} Across
configurations the backbone-equipped models agree on which source is best, and
that agreed ordering is the coverage ordering; coverage is positive from scratch
as well, on real-motion targets (Table~\ref{tab:law}). Wherever the model can
learn the target's features, covering them is what helps. The only place this
breaks is from scratch on semantic targets---and there for a mechanical reason:
the model never learns the task at all.

\noindent\textbf{One regime is out of distribution for the model itself: from
scratch on semantic targets.} This is the single cell where the pattern
inverts---precision predicts ($\dtb$ $+0.47$) and coverage does not ($\dbt$
$-0.16$)---but it is not a second law to explain; it is a regime where the model
never learns the task. Table~\ref{tab:degenerate} shows why. From scratch on
semantic, transfer sits at a floor (${\approx}12$--$14$ \pck{} across
architectures, vs.\ $27$ with a backbone and ${\sim}60$ on real-motion), and a
trained-from-scratch encoder is no better than a frozen \emph{random} one there
($\Delta_{\text{enc}}\le 1.9$ \pck)---training the encoder does not move the model
off the floor. The contrast with real-motion is the tell: the same GLU-Net and
FlowFormer encoders gain $+4.6$ and $+7.7$ \pck{} when trained on real motion, so
they are perfectly capable of learning---semantic correspondence simply is not
learnable from scratch in our setup. These models have converged (their semantic
validation curves plateau or decline, not climb), so this is failure, not
under-training. The reproducible source ranking that survives reflects which
source biases a non-learning model toward the target, not transferable
selection, and we do not rely on it. Because data selection is only meaningful
for a model that learns the task, we exclude this out-of-distribution cell from
the selection analysis of Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}; coverage is the predictor in
every regime where the model learns.

\noindent\textbf{Why direction-blind matching washed out.} Pooled across
configurations and strata, coverage's signal is diluted: averaged with the
off-target direction (near-useless) and with the from-scratch$\times$semantic
cells (negative), it collapses toward the muddy values that symmetric
distribution distances have always returned in this setting. Stratifying and
excluding the one non-learning regime is what brings it out. (A pooled
``regime flip'' between the two directions, which an earlier analysis of ours
reported, is the same artifact; we document it in the supplement for
completeness.)

\noindent\textbf{The result is about coverage, not the estimator.} Replacing
mean-NN with $\epsilon$-radius coverage at 4\,px or 16\,px reproduces the
coverage signal at comparable strength, and even the noisiest estimator we
tried---a directional kNN-KL, which never stabilizes under sampling
(supplement)---shows it attenuated but intact. The support-geometry estimators
are the principled choice for procedural data (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}); that all
three agree shows the finding reflects motion coverage, not an artifact of how
we measure it.

\section{Selecting Training Data at the Reproducibility Ceiling}
\label{sec:predict}

The coverage law licenses a selection rule with zero fitted parameters:
\begin{equation}
\text{rank candidate training sets by motion coverage,}\qquad -\dbt .
\label{eq:rule}
\end{equation}
No training on the target, no target labels, no learned weights---a single
directed distance computed from each candidate's motion histogram against the
target's. The one scope condition is the one of Sec.~\ref{sec:law}: the model
must be able to learn the task, which excludes only the from-scratch$\times$
semantic regime (Table~\ref{tab:degenerate}). Within that scope, coverage is
the best predictor we have, and it ranks candidates at the reproducibility
ceiling of transfer itself (below). It is also the right
generator-\emph{design} objective: because off-target motion is nearly free
(precision $\rho{=}0.13$), one should \emph{add} coverage of the target's
motion rather than \emph{match} its distribution---the move our closed-loop
search exploits (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}).

Table~\ref{tab:predictors} compares coverage against every alternative we
considered, all evaluated with identical within-context machinery on the models
that learn the task. Motion coverage ($\dbt$) ranks at $\rho{=}0.52$---the best
single predictor. The symmetric and distribution distances follow at
$+0.39$--$0.42$ (symmetric mean-NN, sliced Wasserstein-2, FID): they trail
coverage because each folds in off-target mass ($\dtb$), which on its own
carries little signal ($+0.13$), so averaging it into a symmetric score is dead
weight rather than denoising. MMD is weak throughout. The appearance
control---the identical coverage computed in DINO feature space---is
null-to-wrong-signed ($-0.21$): whatever predicts transfer lives in motion, not
in what the frames look like. Restricting to models that learn the task is what
sharpens coverage (from $+0.37$ pooled over all configurations to $+0.52$),
while the symmetric metrics barely move---the precision term they carry is
unhelpful in every regime, not just the excluded one.

\noindent\textbf{Is $\rho{\approx}0.5$ good? It is near the ceiling.}
The right reference is not $\rho{=}1$ but the reproducibility of the ground
truth. Transfer outcomes are noisy measurements: retrain a different model on
the same sources and its source ranking agrees with another's only at
$\rho{\approx}0.59$ (mean pairwise rank-agreement between independent
retrainings on the same contexts---the \emph{empirical oracle}). No predictor
can be validated beyond that agreement, because the target does not agree with
itself more than that. Coverage reaches $\rho{=}0.52$, \textbf{$88\%$ of the
ceiling}: ranking candidates by one motion distance recovers most of what
retraining a sibling model would tell you, at zero training cost and without
rendering a frame.

We also probed for structure a single coverage distance might miss---learned
ridges over the full motion descriptor set, a coverage$\times$off-target
interaction, and a depth-2 gradient-boosted combiner, all fit in-fold and
applied held-out. None beats fit-free coverage (Table~\ref{tab:predictors}, and
supplement): there is nothing for a learned combiner to add over the one
directed distance. Ranking quality translates directly into the decision that
matters---which single source to train on (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/results/F4_gap_stratified.png}
\caption{\textbf{Decision utility tracks the reproducibility of ground
truth.} Probability that ranking by motion coverage picks the truly better of

retraining cannot rank either; above a 10-\pck{} gap coverage is right

\noindent\textbf{What a practitioner gains.}
Translated into decisions, ranking by coverage picks a source within a median
$0.6$ \pck{} of the best available, versus $17$ \pck{} for random selection; on
the pairs that matter---candidates whose true \pck{} differs by more than
$10$---it orders them correctly $77\%$ of the time, matching the agreement of an
independent retraining (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}). Appearance similarity is no better
than random. Below a $2$-\pck{} gap every strategy decays toward chance,
\emph{and so does retraining}: the residual errors are irreducible measurement
noise, not predictor deficiency.

\section{From Ranking to Absolute Prediction}
\label{sec:absolute}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/results/F5_absolute_scatter.png}
\caption{\textbf{Absolute \pck{} prediction} under the three protocols
(predictions $=$ anchor $+$ dispersion-calibrated coverage score; colored by
regime; lines are decile-binned calibration curves). With anchors available
(left), predictions track the diagonal at MAE ${\approx}10$; with both
endpoints unseen (right), the anchor interpolates observed cells through
benchmark-similarity $\times$ source-similarity jointly and coverage
contributes the ranking.}
\label{fig:abs}
\end{figure}

A rank correlation bounds how much \emph{variance} a predictor can explain:
if the coverage score were used as a linear predictor of within-context
transfer, its $R^2$ could not exceed roughly $\rho^2 \approx 0.25$---so even
a predictor at the reproducibility ceiling leaves ${\sim}75\%$ of
within-context variance unexplained, not because the predictor is weak but
because that variance is dominated by run-to-run training noise that no
dataset descriptor can see (Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}). Absolute accuracy
therefore comes from \emph{anchors}---observed runs that pin the scale---and
that division of labor is itself a finding: \textbf{anchors buy units; coverage
buys order}. Concretely
(Fig.~\ref{fig:abs}): with the target benchmark seen (held-out source),
cell-mean anchors plus coverage predict at MAE $9.3$, $r{=}0.88$; per-regime
gain calibration (one scale per regime, fit with the predicted source held
out) yields honestly-dispersed predictions with pretrained-regime MAE
$8.2$ and $r{=}0.90$---accurate enough to budget experiments against. With
\emph{both} endpoints unseen, a two-way kernel anchor (in-fold cells weighted
by benchmark-similarity $\times$ source-similarity) plus the
dispersion-calibrated coverage score reaches $r{\approx}0.5$ in absolute units
while preserving coverage's ranking---the residual magnitude beyond anchors is,
per our calibration-ceiling analysis, feature-unreachable. The supplement
details all anchor and calibration variants, including the failure modes.

\section{Interventions: Steering Generation and Stress-Testing Coverage}
\label{sec:interv}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

(a)~The same sources trained both ways on KITTI-2015: trial19---a tight,
low-coverage source (coverage rank \#9 of 9)---wins from scratch but surrenders

with bit-identical motion: transfer moves $2.7$--$9.4$ \pck{} while appearance

\label{fig:interv}
\end{figure}

Correlational principles should be stressed interventionally. Every result so
far shares one observational table; this section asks two questions of data
that table never touched---a procedurally generated grid (Kubric-derived motion
interventions \cite{greff2021kubric} plus our SDF-fractal generator),
constructed after the analysis of Secs.~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:absolute} was
frozen. \emph{(i)~Is coverage actionable}---can it steer a generator toward a
target before any training? \emph{(ii)~Is the descriptor meaningful}---does
matching it recover physically correct motion, and is motion rather than
appearance what carries transfer? A direct \emph{causal} test of coverage---a
grid that varies coverage at fixed off-target mass---is the one experiment we
have not yet built (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}); the closed-loop result below is the
closest we come.

\noindent\textbf{(i) Closed-loop generation: coverage is actionable.}
A render-free search over generator motion parameters---scored only by how
well the generated motion covers the target's, no training in the loop---produced
a tuned source whose trained margin over the anchor matched prediction on KITTI-2015
($+0.93$ predicted, $+0.87$ realized), while a FlyingThings null control
(predicted ${\approx}0$) realized $-1.6$: no false positive. Selection costs
geometry decoding only; the photorealistic render pass is skipped during
search.

\noindent\textbf{(ii) The recovered motion is physically meaningful
(construct validity).} A natural worry about a descriptor as crude as a 4-D bag of flow
vectors is whether it captures anything real. We test this directly: we run the
same render-free search, but instead of asking ``does this improve transfer?''
we ask ``what motion does matching the descriptor actually produce?'' On a
controllable Kubric-style generator whose parameters $\theta$ explicitly
separate camera motion from object motion, optimizing BFV match alone recovers
the physically correct regime for each target (Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery},
Table~\ref{tab:recovery}): a strong forward dolly with grounded objects for
KITTI (camera-centric), a near-static camera with vertically flying objects for
FlyingThings (object-centric). The recovered camera axis cleanly separates the

motion histogram. An independent generator, matched on BFV alone, lands on the
right physical motion---construct validity earned, not asserted. (BFV is, by
construction, blind to the camera-vs-object \emph{decomposition} when the two
produce identical aggregate flow; we treat that ambiguity in the supplement.)

\noindent\textbf{Appearance vs.\ motion, decomposed.}
The grid's controllability lets us cross the two axes a natural dataset
confounds and read transfer straight off re-rendered models
(Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). Both axes move transfer, by \emph{comparable}
amounts: on KITTI the off-diagonal nearly ties
($91.1$ for good-motion/bad-texture vs.\ $89.5$ for bad-motion/good-texture), so
neither dominates by magnitude. The asymmetry is in \emph{specificity}.
Texture is a target-agnostic tax---HQ beats matte in every cell, the same
direction for every target. Motion structure is target-\emph{specific}: the best
source is whichever motion matches the target, so the winning cell moves from the
camera row for ego-motion KITTI to the object row for object-motion

tells you \emph{which} target it suits. (The magnitude gap does widen as motion
grows badly mismatched---wrong motion \emph{type} costs far more than the
$6$--$10$~\pck{} seen here for wrong \emph{structure}---but within physically
plausible motion the two axes are the same size; we therefore rest the claim on
specificity, not dominance.) Realism is not the targeting axis; matched motion
is.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

We state the coverage law's evidentiary status precisely. \emph{It holds
wherever the model learns the task} and survives every robustness control we
ran---estimator replication, leave-one-benchmark-out, self-pair exclusion, and a
generator-family jackknife (supplement). \emph{The one excluded regime is from
scratch on semantic targets}, where the model does not learn the task at all
(Table~\ref{tab:degenerate}); we treat it as an out-of-distribution model regime,
not a counterexample. That exclusion is observational: we rule out the tempting
alternative that denser or better-spatially-aligned supervision lifts the cell
(source-side density and spatial-coverage features are null there, and
magnitude-equalizing coverage does not move it; supplement), but a clean causal
test---a label-density training ablation---we have not run. One residual wrinkle
within scope: from scratch, off-target mass carries a weak secondary signal,
concentrated in the encoder-degenerate CATs++ (whose trained scratch encoder is
no better than a random one); where the encoder learns, coverage dominates and
off-target mass is near-dead (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}). \emph{Coverage has no causal
test yet}: the closed-loop search steers by coverage and the
predicted gain realizes (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}), but that is suggestive, not a
controlled experiment. A direct test needs a mirror grid---sources searched to
vary coverage at fixed off-target mass---which our render-free search
infrastructure supports building. Ranking at $\rho{\approx}0.5$ caps within-context explainable variance
at ${\sim}25\%$; absolute accuracy beyond anchors is feature-unreachable in our
analysis. Semantic correspondence is the hardest case---a floor from scratch,
and even under a backbone motion descriptors capture only coverage, not full
semantic compatibility. One benchmark (Middlebury) was excluded after we
identified an evaluation-harness defect during analysis; all reported numbers
use the remaining nine (the supplement documents the exclusion and a
sensitivity analysis).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The factor that governs synthetic-to-real correspondence transfer is motion
\emph{coverage}---how well the source covers the target's motion. Ranking
candidate sources by a single directed motion distance sits at the
reproducibility ceiling of transfer itself, beats every symmetric and
distribution distance (which only dilute it with near-useless off-target mass),
carries no signal in appearance space, and predicts absolute performance where
anchors exist---all without training a network or rendering a frame. The only
models it does not serve are those that never learn the task, such as
from-scratch semantic matchers; for them no source-side property helps, and we
say so. Coverage splits cleanly into two uses: \emph{select} natural candidates
by coverage, and \emph{design} generated data by increasing coverage of the
target---and because off-target motion is nearly free, designing means adding
coverage, not matching the distribution, the move our render-free search
realizes on a real target. Because the signal is a property
of the data rather than the renderer, the same rule guides two structurally
different generators---a 3D scene simulator and a procedural implicit-surface
generator---which suggests it is a generator-agnostic layer for deciding
\emph{what} to synthesize, not a property of any one pipeline. While the field
invests heavily in rendering data more realistically and more cheaply, we offer
a complementary and largely orthogonal tool: a target-conditioned answer to what
that data should contain, for correspondence and plausibly for other dense tasks
where training and target distributions can be compared. The recipe is
correspondingly simple: before improving how you render, measure how well your
source covers the target's motion---and remember that a pretrained backbone is
what makes coverage the thing that matters.
