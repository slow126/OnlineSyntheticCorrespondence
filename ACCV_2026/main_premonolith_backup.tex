\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic data trains most correspondence models, so the binding question is no
longer how to render it but which dataset to render for a given target. That
choice is still made by intuition, or by training on several candidates and
keeping what works. We show it can instead be read from a cheap measurement,
with no target labels and no training.

Matching distributions is directional, and only one direction matters: what
predicts transfer is whether a source's motion \emph{covers} the motions the
target uses. Mass on motions the target never needs is nearly free. Symmetric yardsticks like
FID and Wasserstein blur this by averaging both directions, and appearance-based
distances miss it entirely, while a single directed distance over a crude
histogram of flow vectors captures it. Across 142 trained models spanning four
architecture families, ranking candidate sources by this distance is about as
reliable as training a second model to find out: it picks a source within $0.6$
\pck{} of the best, against $17$ for a random pick. Given a few example runs the
same score predicts absolute accuracy, and with no renderer in the loop it tunes
a generator's motion toward a target and beats a generic synthetic dataset.
Cover the motion, not the look.
\keywords{Dense correspondence \and Synthetic training data \and Dataset
selection \and Transferability}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Dense visual correspondence---optical flow, geometric and semantic
matching---relies on synthetic training data more than almost any other vision
task, because dense ground truth is scarce on real images
\cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset,sintel,greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}. Most
progress targets \emph{how} to render this data---fidelity, lighting, speed
\cite{greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}---on the assumption that closing the
appearance gap drives transfer; yet non-photorealistic data often transfers well
\cite{Baradad2021LookingAtNoise,white2009mandelbulb}, and a logically prior
question gets far less attention: given a controllable pipeline, \emph{what}
should it render for a given target? Rendering is cheap; the binding constraint
is deciding what to generate.

The value of a synthetic dataset is not intrinsic; it depends on the
target---the best source for one target is middling for another, so ``best
dataset'' is meaningful only relative to deployment. And matching a source's
distribution to a target's is inherently \emph{directional}: in a shared
motion-descriptor space a source can place mass on motions the target never uses
(\textbf{off-target mass}; precision-analogous) or fail to cover the motions it
needs (\textbf{coverage}/missing support; recall-analogous), which we measure as
the two directed nearest-neighbor distances between the datasets' flow-vector
distributions. Symmetric discrepancies (FID
\cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate}, sliced Wasserstein) average the two;
generative-model evaluation separates them for sample quality
\cite{sajjadi2018assessing_precision_recall,kynkaanniemi2019improved_pr}. We show
that for \emph{transfer} the separation is decisive---one direction carries
almost all the signal:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The coverage principle.} \emph{Matching a source's motion to a target's
helps---but the part that matters is directional. What predicts transfer is
\emph{coverage}: how well the source's motion covers the target's, measured by
the directed distance $\dbt$. Off-target mass---motion the source renders but
the target never uses---barely matters, so one should \emph{cover} the target's
motion rather than \emph{match} its distribution. The exception is models that do
not learn the task at all.}
Two consequences recur: the symmetric and distribution distances (FID,
Wasserstein) trail coverage because they fold the useless direction back in; and
the appearance analogue of coverage carries no target-selective signal:
appearance sets a source's overall quality, not which target it suits.
Throughout, ``pretrained'' refers to the \emph{image-feature backbone} (\eg,
ImageNet ResNet-101), not correspondence-task pre-training; the correspondence
head is always trained on the synthetic source.
\end{quote}

We establish this on a transfer study of 142 trained correspondence models:
four architecture families (CATs++ \cite{cho2022cats++}, RAFT \cite{teed2020raft},
GLU-Net \cite{truong2020glunet}, and FlowFormer \cite{huang2022flowformer}) in 13
backbone configurations over 11 training sources, evaluated on 9 benchmarks
($1{,}278$ transfer measurements). We test it four ways: replication across three
distance estimators; a negative control in appearance-feature space; a
construct-validity check, where matching the descriptor alone recovers each
target's physically correct motion; and a controllable grid that crosses motion
against appearance and shows a target-tuned source beating a generic baseline.
A controlled motion-magnitude grid under a matched protocol maps the coverage
response (inverted-U: under-coverage and over-shoot both cost transfer); a cleaner
test isolating coverage at fixed off-target mass is the one we flag as residual.

We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The coverage law, on every regime that learns the task}
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}): of the distances we test, motion coverage ($\dbt$)
  is the best predictor of correspondence transfer (within-context
  $\rho{=}0.51$, $86\%$ of the reproducibility ceiling) on every architecture
  and training configuration in which the model actually learns. The one
  exception is training from scratch on semantic targets, where the encoder
  never learns the task; we identify this as an out-of-distribution
  \emph{model} regime and set it aside as scope, not as a counterexample
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}).
\item \textbf{A render-free selection rule, zero- and few-shot}
  (Secs.~\ref{sec:predict}--\ref{sec:absolute}): \emph{rank candidate sources by
  motion coverage $\dbt$}: no target-side run, no labels, no learned weights, no
  differentiable renderer. Zero-shot it picks a source within a median $0.6$
  \pck{} of the best (vs.\ $17$ at random) and orders large-gap candidates
  correctly $77\%$ of the time, on par with an independent cross-architecture
  retraining. Absolute \pck{} is set largely by a source-level realism effect the
  motion distances cannot see; given a handful of observed runs as anchors, the
  same score recovers it few-shot (MAE ${\approx}8$--$10$) under leakage-safe
  splits that hold out the source, the benchmark, or both. Anchors buy units;
  coverage buys order.
\item \textbf{Cover, don't match: off-target mass is nearly free}
  (Secs.~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:predict}): off-target mass barely predicts
  transfer ($\dtb$, $\rho{=}0.14$), so the symmetric and distribution
  distances (FID, sliced Wasserstein-2) trail coverage by folding it back
  in, and the appearance analogue of coverage carries no target-selective
  signal (appearance sets a source's overall quality, not which target it
  suits). The prescription is to \emph{add} coverage of the target's motion
  rather than \emph{match} its distribution, which is why broad generic sets
  such as FlyingThings transfer well, and how one can beat them.
\item \textbf{Interventional support} (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}): a render-free
  generator search scored only by motion coverage recovers each target's
  physically correct motion regime (a forward camera dolly for KITTI, object
  motion for FlyingThings; construct validity). A source tuned this way matches
  or beats a generic synthetic baseline in all $8$ settings across two
  architectures, and on a controllable grid crossing motion against appearance,
  motion rather than appearance is the target-specific carrier of transfer.
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit about scope. The coverage law holds wherever the model learns
the task; the one excluded regime, from scratch on semantic targets, fails to
learn the task at all, and we treat it as an out-of-distribution \emph{model}
regime rather than a counterexample (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). The stratification
and exclusion criteria were fixed before FlowFormer entered the study: its four
configurations were added as a held-out fourth architecture family and fell in
line with the coverage law without adjustment (pretrained $\dbt$
$+0.67$/$+0.70$, Table~\ref{tab:law}), a pre-registered check that the analysis
choices are not fit to the data they explain.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Synthetic data for correspondence.}
FlyingChairs/FlyingThings \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset}, Sintel
\cite{sintel}, Kubric/MOVi \cite{greff2021kubric}, and PointOdyssey
\cite{pointodyssey} established synthetic pre-training as the default for
dense matching; AutoFlow \cite{sun2021autoflow} and DFlow \cite{im2022dflow}
\emph{learn} data parameters with training in the loop. Closest to our
premise, Mayer~\etal~\cite{mayer2018what} showed that matching target
displacement statistics improves flow training. That result is undirected and
regime-blind; our finding is the part it could not see. The match is
\emph{directional}: coverage of the target's motion is the half that predicts
transfer, and a pretrained backbone is what lets a model use it. A large, active
line of work improves the \emph{rendering} side of these pipelines (photorealism,
lighting, scale, speed) \cite{greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}; this is
complementary to, but distinct from, our question. We take the renderer as given and instead supply a
target-conditioned signal for \emph{what} to generate, which is why the same
rule guides two structurally different generators (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}).
Procedural abstraction \cite{Baradad2021LookingAtNoise,white2009mandelbulb}
supports our use of non-photorealistic, motion-controllable generators.

\noindent\textbf{Transferability estimation.}
LEEP \cite{nguyen2020leep}, LogME \cite{you2021logme}, NCE
\cite{tran2019transferability}, H-score \cite{bao2019information}, and OTCE
\cite{tan2021otce} predict transfer from model outputs or target labels,
almost exclusively for classification. OTDD \cite{alvarez2020geometric} is,
like ours, a fit-free dataset distance, but symmetric and label-dependent.
None address dense correspondence, none are regime-conditional, and none are
directional; our experiments show the symmetric choice is not a detail but
the reason such signals wash out across regimes.

\noindent\textbf{Precision/recall divergences.}
Directional two-sample diagnostics are established in generative-model
evaluation \cite{sajjadi2018assessing_precision_recall,
kynkaanniemi2019improved_pr,naeem2020reliable,alaa2022faithful}. We adopt the
same decomposition in motion space and make it \emph{predictive and
regime-indexed} for transfer, which to our knowledge is new.

\noindent\textbf{Regime-conditional data value.}
Sorscher~\etal~\cite{sorscher2022beyond} showed the value of easy vs.\ hard
examples flips with data abundance; pretraining-data selection methods such
as DSIR \cite{xie2023data} and DoReMi \cite{xie2023doremi} match
distributions symmetrically. Our result is a new instance of
regime-conditional data value on a new axis (scratch vs.\ pretrained backbone;
directed motion coverage). On the theory side, asymmetric transfer exponents
\cite{hanneke2019value} and covariate-shift support conditions anticipate
our headline---coverage of the target's support is what predicts
transfer. What those arguments do not anticipate is how cheaply it can be
measured (a crude motion histogram, no training, no differentiable renderer) or
that off-target mass is nearly free, so coverage---not symmetric distribution
matching---is the objective. Our dataset-size controls (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}) rule
out the trivial ``more data'' explanation of the source ordering.

\noindent\textbf{Architectures and backbones.}
We span semantic matching (CATs++ \cite{cho2022cats++}), recurrent flow (RAFT
\cite{teed2020raft}), and pyramid dense matching (GLU-Net
\cite{truong2020glunet}); appearance descriptors use DINOv3
\cite{simeoni2025dinov3}, whose strong zero-shot matching
\cite{sd_dino_tale_of_two_features} motivates appearance as a serious
competing hypothesis---which our controls reject for within-context dataset
ranking.

We represent each dataset by the distribution of its motion and ask how well a
training set's distribution covers a target's. This section defines that
representation, the two directed distances that are our central measurement,
and the decomposition we use to turn them into predictions.

\noindent\textbf{Transfer study.}
We study four architecture families: the semantic matcher CATs++
\cite{cho2022cats++}, the optical-flow networks RAFT \cite{teed2020raft} and
FlowFormer \cite{huang2022flowformer}, and the dense-matching network GLU-Net
\cite{truong2020glunet}. For CATs++, GLU-Net, and FlowFormer the image-feature
backbone (ImageNet ResNet-101, VGG, and a pretrained Twins-SVT transformer,
respectively) is either initialized from those features or trained from
scratch, and either frozen or trained jointly with the correspondence head,
giving four configurations each; RAFT is trained from scratch by construction
and is one. The resulting \textbf{13 backbone configurations} are each trained
on 11 pure synthetic sources (\textbf{142 trained models}; one FlowFormer cell
did not complete) and evaluated by peak \pck@0.05 on 9 benchmarks spanning
optical flow, dense, and semantic matching (KITTI-2012/2015 \cite{kitti_flow},
FlyingThings \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset}, PointOdyssey
\cite{pointodyssey}, TSS, PF-Pascal/PF-Willow \cite{pfpascal}, SPair-71k
\cite{spair71k}, and a held-out split of our procedural source), for
\textbf{1{,}278 source--benchmark--configuration transfer measurements}. We
treat each (benchmark, architecture, configuration) triple as a \emph{context}
and make all ranking claims within contexts. Throughout we stratify the nine
benchmarks into \emph{real-motion} targets (KITTI-2012/2015, FlyingThings,
PointOdyssey, and the procedural split), whose ground truth is dense pixel
motion, and \emph{semantic} targets (SPair-71k, PF-Pascal, PF-Willow, TSS),
whose ground truth is sparse keypoint correspondence across object instances;
this distinction is load-bearing for the from-scratch regime.

\noindent\textbf{A degeneracy: training the backbone from scratch.} Three of
these architectures---CATs++, GLU-Net, and FlowFormer---are designed to run on a
\emph{pretrained} image backbone; initializing that backbone randomly and
training it end-to-end with the correspondence head is outside their intended
regime. We include those from-scratch configurations for completeness, and they
expose two failure modes that must be handled before any coverage analysis, both
of which collapse the network to a \emph{static mean prior}---a nearly fixed flow
field that all but ignores which image pair it is given.
First, \textbf{semantic targets collapse without a semantic prior.} A matcher
supervised only on optical-flow-style motion has no route to cross-instance
\emph{semantic} correspondence, so \emph{every} from-scratch architecture
collapses on semantic targets. Training on a \emph{semantic} source instead
(SPair, which one might expect to supply the missing prior) does not rescue it---%
it collapses \emph{worst of all}, never learning to match anything.
Second, \textbf{CATs++ fails to train from scratch outright:} even on real motion
its randomly-initialized backbone mode-collapses, whereas GLU-Net and FlowFormer
learn---and RAFT, the one architecture built to train from scratch with no
pretrained backbone, learns on real motion as expected.

\noindent\textbf{Detecting collapse with source-sensitivity.} We measure this
directly. Holding the target image fixed, we swap the source image and record how
far the predicted flow field moves, relative to its own magnitude---its
\emph{source-sensitivity}. A genuine matcher must move a great deal, since a
different source implies a different correspondence; a model collapsed to a fixed
field barely moves, and its sensitivity falls toward zero.
Table~\ref{tab:staticprior} reports it per architecture, averaged over real-motion
training sources and for the semantic source. The working matchers on real motion
(GLU-Net/FlowFormer, \textbf{bold}) set the healthy scale at $5$--$9$; on semantic
targets every model falls to $\le0.5$, and under the semantic source to
${\approx}0.1$---an order of magnitude lower. The failed models stop tracking
their input.

\noindent\textbf{Excluding the mode-collapsed configurations.} We drop these
configurations from the transfer analysis, treating them as a model--data
incompatibility rather than a result, and on a criterion independent of
coverage---\emph{a trained-from-scratch encoder must beat random features}. Every
excluded cell fails it: the semantic from-scratch cells transfer no better than a
random backbone (they never learn to match), and CATs++ from scratch transfers
\emph{below} random even on real motion ($\Delta_{\text{enc}}<0$;
Table~\ref{tab:degenerate})---the signature of an architecture trained
outside its design regime. This is not undertraining: a higher-learning-rate
schedule only collapses it further. CATs++ on real motion does retain some
source-sensitivity ($2.5^{\dagger}$, hence unshaded), yet still fails the
beat-random test, so we exclude it too. Everything we analyze in
Secs.~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:interv} is a configuration in which the encoder
demonstrably learns to match.

\noindent\textbf{Motion representation.}
We summarize each dataset as a point cloud of flow vectors in a normalized
four-dimensional space of image position and displacement,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{flow}} = [\hat{x},\, \hat{y},\, \Delta\hat{x},\,
\Delta\hat{y}] \in [-1,1]^4,
\label{eq:bfv}
\end{equation}
following the bag-of-flow-vectors representation (Fig.~\ref{fig:splats}). This
retains \emph{where} motion occurs and \emph{how} content moves while remaining
invariant to image resolution and free of any spatial binning. For the
appearance control, we build the analogous representation from DINOv3 patch
features, reduced by PCA and $\ell_2$-normalized \cite{simeoni2025dinov3}.

\noindent\textbf{Two directed distances.}
The match between a training cloud $A$ and a target cloud $B$ is directional,
and we measure each direction by the mean nearest-neighbor distance
\begin{equation}
d_{A \to B} \;=\; \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a \in A}\; \min_{b \in B}\,
\lVert a - b \rVert_2^2 .
\label{eq:dirdist}
\end{equation}
Writing $\dtb$ for the train-to-target direction and $\dbt$ for the reverse,
$\dtb$ is large when much of the training distribution lies far from anything
the target uses (\textbf{off-target mass}), and $\dbt$ is large when target
motions have no nearby training example (\textbf{missing support}).

Each direction can be estimated in several ways, and the choice is not
incidental. Our default, Eq.~(\ref{eq:dirdist}), is a \emph{support-geometry}
estimator: it depends only on how close the two point sets lie, not on their
local densities. We contrast it with two alternatives---$\epsilon$-coverage,
the fraction of one cloud lying within a radius of the other (also
support-based), and a $k$-nearest-neighbor estimate of the KL divergence
(density-based). For procedurally generated data, the support-based estimators
are the principled choice. What matters for transfer is \emph{which} motions a
dataset covers, not how often each one is rendered; yet rendering more frames
of the same motion inflates a density estimate while leaving the support
unchanged, so a density divergence such as KL entangles an intrinsic property
of the data with an artifact of the sampling budget. This is not hypothetical:
the nearest-neighbor and $\epsilon$-coverage estimates converge as the sample
grows, whereas the KL estimate fails to stabilize at any budget we tried
(supp.). Among the three, nearest-neighbor is the most informative predictor
and is our default, $\epsilon$-coverage is close behind, and KL is both the
least stable and the weakest; that all three nonetheless agree on the central
finding (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}) shows it reflects the \emph{direction} of the
comparison rather than the estimator. As direction-blind baselines we also use
the symmetric average of the two directions and two distribution
distances---FID \cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate} and sliced
Wasserstein-2.

\noindent\textbf{Prediction decomposition.}
Absolute transfer accuracy varies far more across benchmarks and architectures
than across training sources, so we separate a context-level term from a
within-context ranking term,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{\pck}}(i,k,v) \;=\; \underbrace{L(k,v)}_{\text{level anchor}}
\;+\; \underbrace{g(i \to k)}_{\text{within-context ranking}},
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is interpolated from observed runs and $g$ ranks sources within a
context. Directionality enters only through $g$, where learning happens; the
anchor $L$ is best interpolated with a direction-blind similarity (supp.).
Crucially, $g$---the zero-shot ranking rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}---fits
nothing and so has nothing to hold out; only the anchor $L$ is interpolated from
data, and only the resulting few-shot absolute predictor is evaluated under
leakage-safe held-out protocols (Sec.~\ref{sec:absolute}).

\noindent\textbf{Correlation convention.} We report Spearman $\rho$ for every
\emph{ranking} claim (which source is better---the quantity the selection rule
acts on) and Pearson $r$ (with MAE) only for \emph{absolute-\pck} prediction
(Sec.~\ref{sec:absolute}). The pooled scatter (Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter}) shows
Pearson $r$ on within-context-standardized values for visualization; the
load-bearing ranking statistic is the Spearman $\rho$ of Table~\ref{tab:law}.

Table~\ref{tab:law} and Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} present the core finding. For
every configuration in which the model learns to match, we correlate transfer
with each directed distance within contexts. One column is the consistent
positive predictor: \emph{coverage} ($\dbt$, bold), how well the source's motion
covers the target's. It is positive and strong wherever the encoder learns---up
to $+0.73$ on real motion, and, under a pretrained backbone, on semantic targets
too (CATs++ $+0.50$, GLU-Net $+0.80$). The mode-collapsed from-scratch
configurations (CATs++ on both strata, and every architecture on semantic
targets) carry no usable signal and are dropped (``--''; identified in
Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}). Across the kept cells, \bst{green} marks the
coverage-family distance that actually correlates \emph{best}: it is coverage
almost everywhere, with a few pretrained-semantic cells where a symmetric distance
(sym or W2) edges it---honest near-ties within the same direction, not a competing
law. Off-target mass ($\dtb$) is erratic by comparison. The same analysis in
DINOv3 appearance space is the negative control (Table~\ref{tab:lawdino}): no
appearance distance is a usable positive predictor anywhere. The target-selective
signal lives in motion, not in what the frames look like.


\noindent\textbf{Excluding the collapsed cells sharpens, not creates, the
result.} The law does not hinge on exactly where we draw the exclusion line.
Keeping every configuration in which the model learns---dropping only
from-scratch$\times$semantic ($77\%$ of the $1{,}278$ measurements)---gives
within-context $\rho{=}0.51$; the stricter ``encoder must beat random features''
criterion ($57\%$ kept; supp.) raises it to $\rho{=}0.57$. A stricter filter
sharpens the finding; it does not manufacture it. (In the collapsed cells a
spurious correlation with off-target mass appears---an artifact of a model that
ignores its input, not a second law; we dissect it in the supplement.)

\noindent\textbf{Why direction-blind matching washed out.} Pooled across
configurations and strata, coverage's signal is diluted: averaged with the
off-target direction (near-useless) and with the non-learning
from-scratch$\times$semantic cells (negative), it collapses toward the muddy
values that symmetric distribution distances return in this setting. Stratifying
and excluding the one non-learning regime is what brings it out.

\noindent\textbf{The result is about coverage, not the estimator.} Replacing
mean-NN with $\epsilon$-radius coverage at 4\,px or 16\,px reproduces the
coverage signal at comparable strength, and even the noisiest estimator we
tried---a directional kNN-KL, which never stabilizes under sampling
(supplement)---shows it attenuated but intact. The support-geometry estimators
are the principled choice for procedural data (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}); that all
three agree shows the finding reflects motion coverage, not an artifact of how
we measure it.

\noindent\textbf{Not an artifact of our synthetic pool or sampling.} The rule
survives a battery of controls, reported in full in the supplement (Robustness
Controls). In brief: dropping any one of the five generator families leaves it
at $\rho\in[0.43,0.54]$ (baseline $0.51$), so no family is load-bearing;
fit-free ranking by dataset size or vector count reaches only $\rho\le0.27$, so
``more data'' does not explain the signal; mean-NN coverage is sampling-invariant
($\rho\ge0.96$ against the largest budget from just $5{\times}10^4$ flow vectors)
whereas density-based estimators never stabilize ($\rho\in[0.2,0.5]$), which is
why we measure support geometry rather than density; and a $200$-permutation
shuffle null sits at $\rho=0.00$.

\section{Selecting Training Data at the Reproducibility Ceiling}
\label{sec:predict}

The coverage law licenses a \emph{zero-shot} selection rule with zero fitted
parameters:
\begin{equation}
\text{rank candidate training sets by motion coverage,}\qquad -\dbt .
\label{eq:rule}
\end{equation}
It is zero-shot in the strict sense: no run on the target, no target labels, no
learned weights, and nothing fit and held out (unlike the absolute predictor of
Sec.~\ref{sec:absolute}). It is a single directed distance computed from each
candidate's motion histogram against the target's.
Ranking is what coverage gives for free; \emph{absolute} \pck{} needs a few
observed runs as anchors (the few-shot extension, Sec.~\ref{sec:absolute}). The one scope condition is the one of Sec.~\ref{sec:law}: the model
must be able to learn the task, which excludes only the from-scratch$\times$
semantic regime (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}). Within that scope, coverage is
the best predictor we have, and it ranks candidates at the reproducibility
ceiling of transfer itself (below). It is also the right
generator-\emph{design} objective: because off-target motion is nearly free
(precision $\rho{=}0.14$), one should \emph{add} coverage of the target's
motion rather than \emph{match} its distribution---the move our render-free
search exploits (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}).

Table~\ref{tab:predictors} runs the same five distances in motion and in
appearance space, so appearance is stress-tested as thoroughly as motion, not
given a single shot. Motion coverage ($\dbt$) ranks at $\rho{=}0.51$---the best
predictor in every column. The symmetric and distribution distances follow at
$+0.39$--$0.42$ (symmetric mean-NN, sliced Wasserstein-2, FID): they trail
coverage because each folds in off-target mass ($\dtb$), which on its own carries
little signal ($+0.14$), so averaging it in is dead weight rather than denoising.
Appearance is the negative control: every DINO distance is null-to-wrong-signed,
the appearance \emph{analogue} of coverage included ($-0.20$). The axis
that selects \emph{which} target a source suits lives in motion; appearance sets
only the source's overall quality.
Restricting to models that learn the task sharpens coverage (from $+0.35$ pooled
over all configurations to $+0.51$) while the symmetric metrics barely move---the
precision term they carry is unhelpful in every regime, not just the excluded
one.

\noindent\textbf{Is $\rho{\approx}0.5$ good? It recovers most of the
data-controllable signal.} Transfer rank varies for two reasons: which
\emph{source} you train on, the part a dataset descriptor can see, and which
\emph{model} you train (architecture, initialization, schedule, size), which it
structurally cannot. We bound the first by how well independent retrainings
\emph{agree} on the source ordering: two models trained on the same sources rank
them alike only at $\rho{\approx}0.59$ (mean pairwise rank-agreement across
independent retrainings---the \emph{empirical oracle}). The variance above that
agreement is not predictor weakness; it is signal that lives in the model, not
the data, and is out of scope for any data-only selection rule. Coverage reaches
$\rho{=}0.51$, \textbf{$86\%$ of this data-controllable ceiling}: ranking
candidates by one motion distance recovers most of what retraining a sibling
model would tell you, at zero training cost and without rendering a frame.

We also probed for structure a single coverage distance might miss---learned
ridges over the full motion descriptor set, a coverage$\times$off-target
interaction, and a depth-2 gradient-boosted combiner, all fit in-fold and
applied held-out. None beats fit-free coverage (Table~\ref{tab:predictors}, and
supplement): there is nothing for a learned combiner to add over the one
directed distance. Ranking quality translates directly into the decision that
matters---which single source to train on (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}).

\noindent\textbf{What a practitioner gains.}
Translated into decisions, ranking by coverage picks a source within a median
$0.9$ \pck{} of the best available, versus ${\sim}15$ \pck{} for random selection; on
the pairs that matter---candidates whose true \pck{} differs by more than
$10$---it orders them correctly $78\%$ of the time, on par with the agreement of
an independent cross-architecture retraining ($81\%$; Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}), all
zero-shot. The wrong directed distance and appearance similarity both stall at
chance on these pairs ($52\%$ and $48\%$). Below a $2$-\pck{} gap every strategy decays toward chance,
\emph{and so does retraining}: the residual errors are irreducible measurement
noise, not predictor deficiency.

\section{From Ranking to Absolute Prediction}
\label{sec:absolute}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/results/F5_absolute_scatter.png}
\caption{\textbf{Absolute \pck{} prediction under the three leakage-safe
protocols} (predicted vs.\ actual peak \pck; dots colored by benchmark, the
red/blue lines the per-regime fits). Predictions are anchor $+$
dispersion-calibrated coverage score. With the source held out (LOTO) the target
benchmark anchors the level and predictions track the diagonal ($r{=}0.86$, MAE
$10.4$); holding out the benchmark (LOBO) widens the band ($r{=}0.64$); with both
endpoints unseen (JOINT) a two-way kernel anchor still recovers the level
($r{=}0.56$). Coverage supplies the ranking throughout
(Table~\ref{tab:protocols}).}
\label{fig:abs}
\end{figure}

Zero-shot coverage orders sources but says nothing about \pck{} in absolute
units. Recovering those is a \emph{few-shot} problem: observe a handful of runs,
borrow the transfer \emph{level} from the nearest of them, and let coverage
supply the \emph{order} within that level. The division of labor is exact---%
\textbf{anchors buy units; coverage buys order}---and we make it precise with a
variance bound, then stress-test the anchor under three leakage-safe protocols.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{\textbf{Few-shot absolute prediction under three leakage-safe
protocols.} Each protocol hides part of the predicted (source, benchmark) pair
from the \emph{anchor} fit; the zero-shot coverage rule itself fits nothing and
is not held out. Coverage $\rho$ is the headline fit-free coverage ranking; because it fits
nothing it is identical across protocols. $r$ and MAE are pooled absolute \pck{}
(anchor $+$ coverage). As the anchor is starved from LOTO to LOBO to JOINT,
absolute error widens but the ranking term holds: anchors buy units, coverage
buys order.}
\label{tab:protocols}
\small
\input{tables/tab_protocols}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Leakage-safe evaluation, and what it tests.} Zero-shot ranking
fits no parameters, so there is nothing to hold out; the few-shot anchor, by
contrast, is interpolated from observed runs and must be stress-tested for
leakage. We evaluate it under three protocols of increasing difficulty, defined
by what is hidden from the anchor fit (Table~\ref{tab:protocols}). \textbf{LOTO}
(leave-one-training-source-out) hides the source and keeps the target benchmark:
generalize to an unseen source on a known target. \textbf{LOBO}
(leave-one-benchmark-out) hides the benchmark and keeps the sources: generalize
to an unseen target from known sources. \textbf{JOINT} hides both, so nothing
about the predicted (source, benchmark) pair has been seen: a fully novel pair.
In every protocol coverage only ranks; the anchor supplies the level.

A rank correlation bounds how much \emph{variance} a predictor can explain: used
as a linear predictor of within-context transfer, the coverage score's $R^2$
cannot exceed roughly $\rho^2 \approx 0.25$, so even a predictor at the
reproducibility ceiling leaves ${\sim}75\%$ of within-context variance
unexplained---run-to-run training noise no dataset descriptor can see
(Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}). Absolute accuracy therefore comes from anchors
(Fig.~\ref{fig:abs}, Table~\ref{tab:protocols}). With the target benchmark seen
(LOTO), anchors plus coverage predict at $r{=}0.86$, MAE $10.4$; calibrating one
gain per regime tightens the pretrained regime to $r{=}0.90$, MAE
$8.2$---accurate enough to budget experiments against. Hiding the benchmark
(LOBO) costs the level but not the order ($r{=}0.64$, MAE $21.8$). With both
endpoints unseen (JOINT), a two-way kernel anchor (in-fold cells weighted by
benchmark-similarity $\times$ source-similarity) plus the dispersion-calibrated
coverage score still reaches $r{=}0.56$ (MAE $29.6$), the within-context residual
collapsing toward zero as expected when no nearby cell is observed. Across all
three protocols the ranking term holds as the anchor degrades; the residual
magnitude beyond anchors is, per our calibration-ceiling analysis,
feature-unreachable. The supplement details every anchor and calibration variant,
including the failure modes.

\section{Interventions: Steering Generation and Stress-Testing Coverage}
\label{sec:interv}

A correlational principle should be stressed interventionally. Every result so
far draws on one observational table, so this section asks three questions of
data that table never touched: a procedurally generated grid (Kubric-derived
motion interventions \cite{greff2021kubric} plus our SDF-fractal generator),
constructed after the analysis of Secs.~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:absolute} was
frozen. \emph{(i)~Is the descriptor meaningful?} Does matching it alone recover
each target's physically correct motion? \emph{(ii)~Is tuning actionable?} Does
a source tuned to cover a target beat a generic synthetic baseline?
\emph{(iii)~Is motion, not appearance, the carrier, and does its coverage
response have the predicted shape?} For (iii) we build a controlled
motion-magnitude $\times$ appearance grid under one matched training protocol
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). Scaling magnitude moves coverage and off-target mass
together, so this maps the coverage \emph{response curve} rather than isolating
coverage at fixed off-target mass; that cleaner isolation remains the one
experiment we flag as residual (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

\noindent\textbf{(i) The recovered motion is physically meaningful
(construct validity).} A descriptor as crude as a 4-D bag of flow vectors
invites a natural worry: does it capture anything real? We test this directly.
We run a render-free search over generator motion parameters, scored only by how
well the generated motion covers the target's (no images, no training in the
loop), and ask what motion matching the descriptor actually produces. On a
controllable Kubric-style generator whose parameters $\theta$ explicitly
separate camera motion from object motion, optimizing BFV match alone recovers
the physically correct regime for each target (Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery},
Table~\ref{tab:recovery}): a strong forward dolly with grounded objects for
KITTI (camera-centric), a near-static camera with vertically flying objects for
FlyingThings (object-centric). The recovered camera axis cleanly separates the
moving-camera target from the static-camera target, with no supervision beyond
the motion histogram. The recovered KITTI source's flow fingerprint bears this
out, a coherent radial-expansion field that captures the target's
forward-ego-motion structure, unlike the isotropic generic anchor
(Fig.~\ref{fig:intervsplats}). An independent generator, matched on BFV alone,
lands on the right physical motion: construct validity earned, not asserted. One
caveat: BFV is by construction blind to the camera-vs-object \emph{decomposition}
when the two produce identical aggregate flow, since an ego-motion dolly and
flying objects can share an aggregate fingerprint. We therefore read the
camera/object split off $\theta$, not off the splat, and treat the ambiguity in
the supplement.

\noindent\textbf{(ii) A tuned source beats a generic baseline.}
The recovered motion is not just physically correct; it also trains well. We
materialize the KITTI-recovered source (GSO assets, HDRI lighting) and train on
it. It matches or beats MOVi-F \cite{greff2021kubric}, the standard generic
Kubric baseline, in \emph{all eight} KITTI settings across two architectures
(Table~\ref{tab:interv}). CATs++ improves by $+8.3$/$+1.9$ from scratch and
$+2.7$/$+1.8$ with a pretrained backbone; GLU-Net improves by $+13.3$/$+7.0$
from scratch and by a striking $+22.5$/$+16.1$ with a pretrained backbone
(KITTI-2012/2015). The two CATs++-scratch wins come from prior-matching, not
learned features: that configuration is degenerate (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}),
so a source whose motion marginal sits closer to the target transfers better
even though the encoder never learns (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}). We compare source and
baseline at their best checkpoint (peak \pck) under matched training, so neither
is penalized by schedule length. A target-specific source built without a single
real-target label thus beats a generic synthetic dataset.

\noindent\textbf{(iii) Motion is the carrier, and over-shooting it costs: the
coverage response is non-monotonic.} The experiments above turn on motion
\emph{type}; we close by varying motion \emph{amount}. Holding scene, objects, and training protocol
fixed, we scale the camera-dolly magnitude across five rungs ($0.25\times$ to
$2\times$ KITTI's) and cross it with texture, training GLU-Net from scratch on
each of the $20$ sources and reading peak \pck{} on KITTI
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). Transfer is \emph{inverted-U} in magnitude, peaking at
the target-matched rung ($1\!-\!1.5\times$) on every target and texture. The two
arms are the recall/precision asymmetry made causal: \emph{under-coverage}
($0.25\times$) leaves the target's larger motions uncovered and transfer is low;
raising magnitude toward the target lifts coverage and transfer (the recall arm).
Past the match, magnitude keeps the target covered (coverage at ceiling) while
adding motion the target never uses, and transfer \emph{falls}---a near-isolated
read on off-target mass, since coverage is saturated across the over-shoot arm. A
$2\times$ over-shoot costs $5$--$11$ \pck{} from scratch. Texture is again a fixed
tax (matte below photoreal at every rung) and the two interact: from scratch matte
cannot absorb a gross over-shoot (matte/$2\times$ falls to $82.9$ on KITTI-2012
vs.\ photoreal's $91.6$), though a pretrained backbone restores the tolerance
(supp.). The controlled response thus \emph{refines} the observational law rather
than contradicting it---coverage is the lever and off-target mass is near-free
among plausible (natural) sources, but deliberate over-shoot is the boundary where
off-target mass begins to bite.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

We state the coverage law's evidentiary status precisely. \emph{It holds
wherever the model learns the task} and survives every robustness control we
ran---estimator replication, leave-one-benchmark-out, self-pair exclusion, and a
generator-family jackknife (supplement). \emph{The one excluded regime is from
scratch on semantic targets}, where the model does not learn the task at all
(Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}); we treat it as an out-of-distribution model regime,
not a counterexample. That exclusion is observational: we rule out the tempting
alternative that denser or better-spatially-aligned supervision lifts the cell
(source-side density and spatial-coverage features are null there, and
magnitude-equalizing coverage does not move it; supplement), but a clean causal
test---a label-density training ablation---we have not run. One residual wrinkle
within scope: from scratch, off-target mass carries a weak secondary signal,
concentrated in the encoder-degenerate CATs++ (whose trained scratch encoder
transfers below a random one); where the encoder learns, coverage dominates and
off-target mass is near-dead (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}). \emph{The causal evidence is a response curve, not a fixed-off-target
isolation}: the render-free search recovers the correct motion regime and a tuned
source beats a generic baseline in every setting tested, and a controlled
motion-magnitude grid (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}) shows
transfer is inverted-U in magnitude under one matched protocol---coverage lifts
transfer up to the match, over-shoot lowers it. Because magnitude moves coverage
and off-target mass together, this maps the response rather than isolating
coverage at fixed off-target mass; that cleaner isolation---a mirror grid varying
coverage breadth alone---remains future work our render-free infrastructure
supports. Absolute accuracy beyond
anchors is feature-unreachable in our analysis (Sec.~\ref{sec:absolute}).
Semantic correspondence is the hardest case---a floor from scratch,
and even under a backbone motion descriptors capture only coverage, not full
semantic compatibility. One benchmark (Middlebury) was excluded after we
identified an evaluation-harness defect during analysis; all reported numbers
use the remaining nine (the supplement documents the exclusion and a
sensitivity analysis).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

What predicts synthetic-to-real correspondence transfer is motion
\emph{coverage}: how well a source covers the target's motion. That a source
should move like its target is not a new idea; what we add is that the useful
form of it is directional, and cheap to check. Ranking candidate sources by one
directed motion distance already sits at the reproducibility ceiling of transfer.
It beats every symmetric and distribution distance, which only dilute the signal
with near-useless off-target mass, and it has no appearance-space analogue: how
the frames look does not tell you which target a source suits. None of this needs
training a network or rendering a frame.

Because off-target motion is nearly free, coverage does two jobs. To
\emph{select} among existing sources, rank them by coverage. To \emph{design} new
data, add coverage of the target instead of matching its distribution, the move
our render-free search exploits on a real target. The same rule guided two very
different generators, a 3D scene simulator and a procedural implicit-surface
generator, so it is a property of the data, not of any one renderer. It does not
serve models that never learn the task, such as from-scratch semantic matchers,
and we say so. The recipe is short. Before you improve how you render, measure
how well your source covers the target's motion; a pretrained backbone is what
lets the model act on it.
