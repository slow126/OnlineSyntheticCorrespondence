\begin{abstract}
Correspondence tasks---dense optical flow, geometric matching, and semantic
matching---rely heavily on synthetic training data, but relatively little
attention has gone to a prior question: given a synthetic generator,
\emph{what} should it render for a particular target?
We study this across dense, geometric, and semantic correspondence and find
that motion coverage---how
well a source covers the motions a target uses---is the most reliable predictor
of transfer, while appearance similarity carries little target-selective signal.
Standard symmetric distances (FID, sliced Wasserstein) blend two asymmetric
failure modes: missing coverage and excess off-target mass.
Decomposing into the two directed halves recovers sharper and more stable signal,
with the coverage half driving the finding.
Across 76 trained models spanning four architecture families, a single directed
distance over a compact flow-vector representation ranks training sources about
as reliably as training a second model from scratch.
Given a few reference runs it also predicts absolute performance.
A render-free generator search, scored only on motion, tunes a synthetic source
to outperform a generic Kubric baseline on target benchmarks.
Cover the motion, not the look.

\keywords{Dense correspondence \and Synthetic training data \and Dataset
selection \and Transferability}
\end{abstract}
