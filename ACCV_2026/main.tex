\begin{abstract}
Dense correspondence---optical flow, geometric and semantic matching---relies
heavily on synthetic training data. Most effort targets \emph{how} realistically a
source renders; we study a prior question about its \emph{motion} for a given
target---not which objects a scene contains, but how they move. Across dense,
geometric, and semantic correspondence we find that motion, not appearance, is the
reliable predictor of transfer: how well a
source's motion \emph{covers} the motions a target uses predicts transfer, while
appearance similarity carries little target-selective signal. Coverage is one
direction of a Chamfer distance between the datasets' flow distributions; the other
direction---rendering motion the target never uses---costs almost nothing, so
symmetric distances (FID, sliced Wasserstein) that average the two only dilute the
signal. Across 76 trained models spanning four architecture families and 684
transfer measurements, ranking sources by this single directed distance is about as
reliable as training a second model to rank them, and given a few reference runs it
also predicts absolute accuracy. Because coverage can be scored from a
geometry-only pass---the scene's motion, with no textures or lighting---and without
training a network, a cheap derivative-free search tunes a controllable generator's
motion to a target and beats a generic Kubric baseline; the same objective steers
two structurally different generators, so it is renderer-agnostic.
Cover the motion, not the look.

\keywords{Dense correspondence \and Synthetic training data \and Dataset
selection \and Transferability}
\end{abstract}
