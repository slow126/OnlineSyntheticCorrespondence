\begin{abstract}
Visual correspondence (optical flow, dense geometric matching, and semantic keypoint
matching) depends on synthetic training data, and most work asks how realistically a
source should render. We ask instead whether the motion a source generates matters
for a given target, how much, and what motion to render. Representing a dataset's
motion as a distribution over its flow vectors, we find the predictive quantity is
directional. How well a source's motion \emph{covers} the target's, one directed half
of a Chamfer distance, predicts transfer across all three tasks, while the reverse
half, motion the source renders but the target never uses, carries almost no signal.
Symmetric distances (FID, sliced Wasserstein) average the two and dilute it, and
appearance similarity carries no target-selective signal at all. Coverage becomes a
practical rule. Ranking sources by this single directed distance needs no training
and no target labels, is about as reliable as training a second model to rank them,
and sits near the upper bound set by transfer's own reproducibility. A few reference runs
extend it to an absolute accuracy estimate. Because coverage is computed from
geometry alone, with no rendering or model training, a derivative-free search also
uses it to \emph{design} data, steering two very different generators, the
photorealistic Kubric and an asset-free SDF-Fractals3D, toward a target and improving
transfer. That the same objective works for both is evidence the signal is a property
of the data, not the renderer.

\keywords{Dense correspondence \and Synthetic training data \and Dataset
selection \and Transferability}
\end{abstract}
