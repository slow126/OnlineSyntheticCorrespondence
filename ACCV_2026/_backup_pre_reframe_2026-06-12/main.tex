\title{Off-Target Mass or Missing Support? Training Regime Determines Which
Direction of Motion Coverage Governs Synthetic-to-Real Correspondence Transfer}
\titlerunning{Off-Target Mass or Missing Support?}

\author{Anonymous ACCV 2026 Submission}
\authorrunning{ACCV-26 submission ID *****}
\institute{Paper ID *****}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic data is the default for training visual correspondence models, but
choosing which synthetic dataset will transfer best to a given target is still
largely guesswork. We show the choice can be made by measurement. A training
set can mismatch a target's motion in two ways---by placing mass on motions
the target never uses (\emph{off-target mass}), or by failing to cover
motions the target needs (\emph{missing support})---and across 891 transfer
evaluations (3 architectures in 9 training configurations $\times$ 11
synthetic training sets $\times$ 9 benchmarks; 99 trained models) we find
that the training regime sets which of these two costs a model pays. \emph{Training from scratch pays for both}: candidate
quality tracks the sum of the two directed distances. \emph{A pretrained
image-feature backbone (\eg, an ImageNet ResNet) forgives off-target mass},
leaving missing support as the sole cost---and because the two directions
trade off across real generators (tight sources waste little but cover
little), averaging the forgiven term back in corrupts pretrained rankings
(symmetric $\rho\,{=}\,{+}0.33$ vs.\ ${+}0.76$ for missing support alone on
the worst-served architecture). The observable signature---which single
direction predicts better flips with regime---holds in 8 of 9 training
variants (exact permutation $p{=}0.029$), replicates across distance
estimators, and vanishes in appearance space ($p{=}0.43$); per-regime
regressions independently recover the same sign structure---both costs
load from scratch, missing support dominates under a backbone---and the
fit-free policy they imply transfers across architectures where fancier
combiners do not.

The resulting fit-free, regime-aware selection policy---rank candidates by
the summed distance from scratch, by missing support under a backbone---ranks
held-out sources, benchmarks, and both at $\rho{\approx}0.5$: parity with
retraining a same-regime sibling variant, $73$--$76\%$ of a
three-architecture retraining consensus, and a median 1.2--2.8 PCK of top-1
selection regret versus 13.9 at random; the identical machinery on
appearance features carries no signal. On a controlled generated grid the
analysis never saw, the off-target cost ranks never-seen sources
out-of-sample where it varies substantially ($\rho{=}{+}0.67$) while the
symmetric average does not. A render-free
generator search realizes its predicted gain on a real target; the
pretrained half remains a stated, falsifiable prediction. The takeaway:
measure both directions of motion mismatch before rendering---from scratch
you pay for both, but bring a backbone and only missing support matters.
\keywords{Dense correspondence \and Synthetic training data \and Dataset
selection \and Transferability}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Dense visual correspondence---optical flow, geometric and semantic
matching---relies on synthetic training data more than almost any other vision
task, because dense ground truth is scarce and expensive to obtain on real
images \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset,sintel,greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}.
Most recent progress targets \emph{how} to render this data---higher fidelity,
better lighting, faster and lighter pipelines
\cite{greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}---on the assumption that narrowing the
appearance gap to real images is what drives transfer, an assumption already
complicated by abstract, non-photorealistic data that transfers surprisingly
well \cite{Baradad2021LookingAtNoise,white2009mandelbulb}. A logically prior
question has received far less attention: given a controllable pipeline,
\emph{what} should it render for a particular target? Rendering is now cheap;
the binding constraint is deciding what to generate, and for whom.

A structural difficulty underlies this question: the value of a synthetic
dataset is not a property of the dataset alone. The same source can rank first
among candidates under one training setup and middle-of-the-pack under another,
with nothing changed but whether the model's image-feature backbone is
pretrained. We trace this to a simple fact: comparing a training distribution
to a target distribution is inherently \emph{directional}. In a shared motion
descriptor space, a training set can mismatch a target in two distinct ways---it
can place mass on motions the target never uses (\textbf{off-target mass};
precision-analogous), or fail to cover motions the target requires
(\textbf{missing support}; recall-analogous)---which we measure as the two
directed nearest-neighbor distances between the datasets' flow-vector
distributions. Symmetric discrepancies---FID
\cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate}, MMD
\cite{gretton2008kernelmethodtwosampleproblem}, sliced Wasserstein---average
these two failure modes together. Generative-model evaluation separates them to
assess sample quality \cite{sajjadi2018assessing_precision_recall,kynkaanniemi2019improved_pr};
we show that for predicting \emph{transfer} the separation is not merely
diagnostic: which direction matters is determined by the training regime:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The regime--direction principle.} \emph{Training from scratch pays
for both kinds of mismatch---capacity is spent on motions the target never
uses ($\dtb$), and motions the target needs go unsupervised ($\dbt$)---so a
candidate's cost is their \textbf{sum}. A pretrained image-feature backbone
forgives off-target mass, leaving missing support as the \textbf{sole}
cost.} The observable signature, which we test throughout, is a flip: which
single direction is the more informative predictor switches with the
regime---and because the two directions trade off across natural sources
(tight sources waste little but cover little), including the forgiven term
under a backbone actively corrupts the ranking. Throughout, ``pretrained''
refers to the \emph{image-feature backbone} (\eg, ImageNet ResNet-101), not
to correspondence-task pre-training; the correspondence head is always
trained on the synthetic source.
\end{quote}

We establish this on a transfer study of 99 trained correspondence
models---3 architectures (CATs++ \cite{cho2022cats++}, RAFT
\cite{teed2020raft}, GLU-Net \cite{truong2020glunet}) in 9 backbone
configurations $\times$ 11 training sources, evaluated on 9 benchmarks
(891 transfer measurements)---and test
it four independent ways: replication across three distance estimators, a
negative control in appearance-feature space, a pre-registered out-of-sample
test on a controlled generated grid (which the from-scratch half passes; the
pretrained half is not yet testable there and we say so), and a closed-loop
intervention in which the signal steers a generator and training confirms the
predicted gain.

We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The regime--direction principle} (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}): the
  training regime sets which mismatch costs a model pays---both directions
  from scratch, missing support alone under a pretrained backbone. Its
  observable signature, which single directed distance is the \emph{more
  informative} predictor, flips with regime in 8 of 9 variants, the ninth
  direction-neutral (exact permutation $p{=}0.029$; benchmark-bootstrap CIs
  exclude zero on both sides of the flip), and is motion-specific: the
  identical analysis in DINO appearance space shows none ($p{=}0.43$).
\item \textbf{A fit-free selection policy at the reproducibility ceiling}
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}): \emph{rank candidates by $\dtb{+}\dbt$ when
  training from scratch; by $\dbt$ alone under a pretrained backbone.} It
  ranks held-out sources, benchmarks, and both at $\rho \approx 0.5$---best
  of every predictor we evaluate, at parity with same-regime retraining
  agreement in the pretrained regime, $73$--$76\%$ of a three-architecture
  retraining consensus---and picks training sets at a median top-1 regret of
  $1.2$--$2.8$ \pck{} vs.\ $13.9$ random, while the identical analysis on
  DINO appearance features carries no within-context signal.
\item \textbf{Why direction-blindness fails asymmetrically}
  (Secs.~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:predict}): the two directed distances
  trade off across natural sources (within-context $\rho \approx -0.37$),
  so symmetric distances---which charge both costs in every regime---are
  competitive exactly where both costs are real (from scratch) and discard
  up to half the signal where one is forgiven (pretrained,
  ${+}0.76$ vs.\ ${+}0.33$). Per-regime regressions independently recover
  the cost structure (both costs load from scratch, recall-dominant
  pretrained), and a leave-one-architecture-out test shows these are the \emph{only}
  combiners that transfer across architectures---interactions, hierarchies,
  and boosted models do not improve on them.
\item \textbf{Ranking zero-shot, calibration only with anchors}
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:absolute}): the policy ranks candidates without any target-side
  training, but absolute \pck{} is not recoverable from motion statistics
  alone---it is set largely by a source-level appearance/realism effect the
  motion distances do not see. Given a few observed runs as anchors, the policy
  score interpolates absolute \pck{} (MAE ${\approx}9{-}10$ held-out;
  $r{=}0.90$ in the pretrained regime); absent anchors we claim ranking only.
\item \textbf{Interventional support} (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}): on a
  generated grid the analysis never saw, the off-target cost ranks
  never-seen from-scratch sources where it varies substantially
  (FlyingThings, $\rho{=}{+}0.67$, against the symmetric average's $-0.28$);
  a render-free generator search improves a real-data target as predicted,
  with a null control; and appearance-stripped twins with bit-identical
  motion isolate motion as the carrier of transfer.
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit about scope: the principle is established observationally on
nine training variants and validated out-of-sample for the from-scratch
regime only; the from-scratch GLU-Net variant is direction-neutral; the
pretrained evidence spans two backbone architectures; and ranking at
$\rho{\approx}0.5$ caps within-context explainable variance at
${\sim}25\%$---absolute accuracy beyond that comes from anchors, not
features (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). The policy's two arms also have distinct
jobs: the summed distance \emph{selects} among natural candidates, whose two
costs covary, while generator \emph{design} must move the regime's binding
direction specifically (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Synthetic data for correspondence.}
FlyingChairs/FlyingThings \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset}, Sintel
\cite{sintel}, Kubric/MOVi \cite{greff2021kubric}, and PointOdyssey
\cite{pointodyssey} established synthetic pre-training as the default for
dense matching; AutoFlow \cite{sun2021autoflow} and DFlow \cite{im2022dflow}
\emph{learn} data parameters with training in the loop. Closest to our
premise, Mayer~\etal~\cite{mayer2018what} showed that matching target
displacement statistics improves flow training. That result is undirected and
regime-blind; our central finding is precisely the part it could not see: the
\emph{direction} of the match is what matters, and the regime selects it.
A large, active line of work improves the \emph{rendering} side of these
pipelines---photorealism, lighting, scale, and speed
\cite{greff2021kubric,pointodyssey}; this is complementary to, but distinct
from, our question. We take the renderer as given and instead supply a
target-conditioned signal for \emph{what} to generate, which is why the same
rule guides two structurally different generators (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}).
Procedural abstraction \cite{Baradad2021LookingAtNoise,white2009mandelbulb}
supports our use of non-photorealistic, motion-controllable generators.

\noindent\textbf{Transferability estimation.}
LEEP \cite{nguyen2020leep}, LogME \cite{you2021logme}, NCE
\cite{tran2019transferability}, H-score \cite{bao2019information}, and OTCE
\cite{tan2021otce} predict transfer from model outputs or target labels,
almost exclusively for classification. OTDD \cite{alvarez2020geometric} is,
like ours, a fit-free dataset distance, but symmetric and label-dependent.
None address dense correspondence, none are regime-conditional, and none are
directional; our experiments show the symmetric choice is not a detail but
the reason such signals wash out across regimes.

\noindent\textbf{Precision/recall divergences.}
Directional two-sample diagnostics are established in generative-model
evaluation \cite{sajjadi2018assessing_precision_recall,
kynkaanniemi2019improved_pr,naeem2020reliable,alaa2022faithful}. We adopt the
same decomposition in motion space and make it \emph{predictive and
regime-indexed} for transfer, which to our knowledge is new.

\noindent\textbf{Regime-conditional data value.}
Sorscher~\etal~\cite{sorscher2022beyond} showed the value of easy vs.\ hard
examples flips with data abundance; pretraining-data selection methods such
as DSIR \cite{xie2023data} and DoReMi \cite{xie2023doremi} match
distributions symmetrically. Our result is a new instance of
regime-conditional data value on a new axis (scratch vs.\ pretrained backbone;
directed motion coverage). On the theory side, asymmetric transfer exponents
\cite{hanneke2019value} and covariate-shift support conditions anticipate the
\emph{recall} half of our principle for backbone-equipped training; the \emph{precision}
half---that off-target mass is actively harmful from scratch, not merely
wasted---is, to our knowledge, theoretically unanticipated, and our
dataset-size controls (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}) rule out the trivial explanation.

\noindent\textbf{Architectures and backbones.}
We span semantic matching (CATs++ \cite{cho2022cats++}), recurrent flow (RAFT
\cite{teed2020raft}), and pyramid dense matching (GLU-Net
\cite{truong2020glunet}); appearance descriptors use DINOv3
\cite{simeoni2025dinov3}, whose strong zero-shot matching
\cite{sd_dino_tale_of_two_features} motivates appearance as a serious
competing hypothesis---which our controls reject for within-context dataset
ranking.

\section{Setup: Two Directed Distances and a Decomposition}
\label{sec:setup}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/splats/composite_overview.png}
\caption{\textbf{Motion fingerprints (BFV splats).} Each panel aggregates a
dataset's joint flow vectors $\mathbf{f}=[\hat x,\hat y,\Delta\hat
x,\Delta\hat y]$, clusters them spatially (non-uniform, data-driven
clusters), and renders each cluster as a directional splat: color encodes
dominant flow direction (legend), splat extent encodes the cluster's spread.
The first three columns hold the same dataset in both splits (train above
eval); their fingerprints match, confirming the signature is a property of the
data rather than of a particular split. Training sets and benchmarks otherwise
occupy visibly different regions of motion space; the two directed distances
defined below quantify the two distinct ways their supports can fail to align.}
\label{fig:splats}
\end{figure}

We represent each dataset by the distribution of its motion and ask how well a
training set's distribution covers a target's. This section defines that
representation, the two directed distances that are our central measurement,
and the decomposition we use to turn them into predictions.

\noindent\textbf{Transfer study.}
We study three architectures: the semantic matcher CATs++
\cite{cho2022cats++}, the optical-flow network RAFT \cite{teed2020raft}, and
the dense-matching network GLU-Net \cite{truong2020glunet}. For CATs++ and
GLU-Net, the image-feature backbone is either initialized from foundation
features (ImageNet ResNet-101 and VGG, respectively) or trained from scratch,
and either frozen or trained jointly with the correspondence head, giving four
variants each; RAFT is trained from scratch by construction and is one
variant. The resulting \textbf{9 variants} are each trained on 11 pure
synthetic sources (99 trained models) and evaluated by peak \pck@0.05 on 9
benchmarks spanning
optical flow, dense, and semantic matching (KITTI-2012/2015 \cite{kitti_flow},
FlyingThings \cite{mayer2016FlyingThingsDataset}, PointOdyssey
\cite{pointodyssey}, TSS, PF-Pascal/PF-Willow \cite{pfpascal}, SPair-71k
\cite{spair71k}, and a held-out split of our procedural source), for
\textbf{891 source--benchmark--variant transfer measurements}. We treat each (benchmark, architecture,
variant) triple as a \emph{context} and make all ranking claims within
contexts.

\noindent\textbf{Motion representation.}
We summarize each dataset as a point cloud of flow vectors in a normalized
four-dimensional space of image position and displacement,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{flow}} = [\hat{x},\, \hat{y},\, \Delta\hat{x},\,
\Delta\hat{y}] \in [-1,1]^4,
\label{eq:bfv}
\end{equation}
following the bag-of-flow-vectors representation (Fig.~\ref{fig:splats}). This
retains \emph{where} motion occurs and \emph{how} content moves while remaining
invariant to image resolution and free of any spatial binning. For the
appearance control, we build the analogous representation from DINOv3 patch
features, reduced by PCA and $\ell_2$-normalized \cite{simeoni2025dinov3}.

\noindent\textbf{Two directed distances.}
The match between a training cloud $A$ and a target cloud $B$ is directional,
and we measure each direction by the mean nearest-neighbor distance
\begin{equation}
d_{A \to B} \;=\; \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a \in A}\; \min_{b \in B}\,
\lVert a - b \rVert_2^2 .
\label{eq:dirdist}
\end{equation}
Writing $\dtb$ for the train-to-target direction and $\dbt$ for the reverse,
$\dtb$ is large when much of the training distribution lies far from anything
the target uses (\textbf{off-target mass}), and $\dbt$ is large when target
motions have no nearby training example (\textbf{missing support}).

Each direction can be estimated in several ways, and the choice is not
incidental. Our default, Eq.~(\ref{eq:dirdist}), is a \emph{support-geometry}
estimator: it depends only on how close the two point sets lie, not on their
local densities. We contrast it with two alternatives---$\epsilon$-coverage,
the fraction of one cloud lying within a radius of the other (also
support-based), and a $k$-nearest-neighbor estimate of the KL divergence
(density-based). For procedurally generated data, the support-based estimators
are the principled choice. What governs transfer is \emph{which} motions a
dataset covers, not how often each one is rendered; yet rendering more frames
of the same motion inflates a density estimate while leaving the support
unchanged, so a density divergence such as KL entangles an intrinsic property
of the data with an artifact of the sampling budget. This is not hypothetical:
the nearest-neighbor and $\epsilon$-coverage estimates converge as the sample
grows, whereas the KL estimate fails to stabilize at any budget we tried
(supp.). Among the three, nearest-neighbor is the most informative predictor
and is our default, $\epsilon$-coverage is close behind, and KL is both the
least stable and the weakest; that all three nonetheless agree on the central
finding (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}) shows it reflects the \emph{direction} of the
comparison rather than the estimator. As direction-blind baselines we also use
the symmetric average of the two directions and three distribution
distances---FID \cite{heusel2018ganstrainedtimescaleupdate}, sliced
Wasserstein-2, and MMD \cite{gretton2008kernelmethodtwosampleproblem}.

\noindent\textbf{Prediction decomposition.}
Absolute transfer accuracy varies far more across benchmarks and architectures
than across training sources, so we separate a context-level term from a
within-context ranking term,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{\pck}}(i,k,v) \;=\; \underbrace{L(k,v)}_{\text{level anchor}}
\;+\; \underbrace{g(i \to k)}_{\text{within-context ranking}},
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is interpolated from observed runs and $g$ ranks sources within a
context. Directionality enters only through $g$, where learning happens; the
anchor $L$ is best interpolated with a direction-blind similarity (supp.). We
report three leakage-safe protocols of increasing difficulty---holding out the
source, holding out the benchmark, and holding out both---so that for the
hardest protocol nothing about a predicted (source, benchmark) pair has been
seen during fitting.

Table~\ref{tab:law} and Fig.~\ref{fig:law} present the core finding. For
every training variant we correlate transfer with each directed distance
within contexts and ask \emph{which direction carries the signal}. The
pattern is an interaction, easiest to see in the direction plane of
Fig.~\ref{fig:law}b: all four backbone-pretrained variants sit well above
the diagonal (missing support more informative), four of five from-scratch
variants on or below it (off-target mass more informative), with from-scratch
GLU-Net the single, marginal exception. Group means: scratch
$\rho[\dtb]{=}{+}0.42$ vs.\ $\rho[\dbt]{=}{+}0.23$; pretrained-backbone
$\rho[\dbt]{=}{+}0.61$ vs.\ $\rho[\dtb]{=}{-}0.08$. The regime difference in
direction preference is the most extreme value attainable under its exact
permutation test ($p{=}0.0286$ excluding RAFT from the labeling; $p{=}0.0079$
with RAFT in the scratch group); leave-one-benchmark-out never weakens it
below ${\sim}90\%$ of its value, and excluding self-pairs (a training set evaluated
on its own held-out split) leaves it intact.

\noindent\textbf{The two directions trade off across sources---which is why
summing them is right from scratch and wrong under a backbone.}
Across the canonical sources the two directed distances are
\emph{anti-correlated} within contexts (mean Spearman $\approx{-}0.37$): a
tight, concentrated source wastes little motion but covers little, a broad
one the reverse. This single fact organizes the right-hand columns of
Table~\ref{tab:law}. From scratch, both failure modes cost, so the natural
candidate score is the \emph{total} cost $\dtb{+}\dbt$---which is exactly the
symmetric average---and indeed it is the best single number for most
from-scratch variants (group mean $+0.50$ vs.\ $+0.42$ for $\dtb$ alone):
the two terms carry complementary halves of the cost, not redundant copies
of one signal. Under a pretrained backbone the off-target cost is forgiven,
and summing it back in is now actively destructive, \emph{because} of the
trade-off: the forgiven term is anti-correlated with the real one, so the
symmetric mean is dragged from the matched direction's $+0.61$ down to
$+0.39$, and in the strongest cells (pretrained GLU-Net) it discards half
the signal ($+0.26$/$+0.33$ vs.\ $+0.67$/$+0.76$). The same reading makes a
falsifiable prediction for designed data: hold the coverage cost flat and
vary only off-target mass, and the from-scratch total cost reduces to the
off-target term---the directed distance should keep predicting while the
symmetric average, half of whose input is now near-constant noise, should
not. The generated grid of Sec.~\ref{sec:interv} confirms this on the one
benchmark where that contrast is clean (FlyingThings: directed $+0.67$,
symmetric $-0.28$).

\noindent\textbf{The principle is about the direction, not the estimator.}
Replacing mean-NN with $\epsilon$-radius coverage at 4\,px or 16\,px
reproduces the flip at comparable strength ($+0.65$, $+0.91$), and even the
noisiest estimator we tried---a directional kNN-KL, which never stabilizes
under sampling (supplement)---shows the same flip, attenuated ($+0.30$); all
at the permutation floor $p{=}0.0286$. \textbf{And it is motion-specific:} running the
identical machinery in DINOv3 appearance space yields a flip statistic of
$+0.018$ ($p{=}0.43$), appearance correlations that are weak-to-negative in
both directions for every variant, and leave-one-benchmark-out ranges that
cross zero (Table~\ref{tab:lawdino}, Fig.~\ref{fig:dino}). Whatever drives
the flip lives in motion structure, not in what the frames look like.

\noindent\textbf{Mechanism (interpretation).}
The two-cost reading is the simplest account we know that fits every
observation at once. From-scratch learners allocate capacity across whatever
they are fed \emph{and} can only learn motions they are shown---so both
off-target mass and missing support cost, and the total $\dtb{+}\dbt$ ranks
candidates. Pretrained representations absorb distributional excess---the
off-target cost is forgiven---but cannot supply target motion never seen
during adaptation, leaving $\dbt$ as the sole cost; with the two distances
anti-correlated across sources, this also explains why $\dtb$'s correlation
turns \emph{negative} under a backbone rather than merely fading.
Covariate-shift theory anticipates the missing-support cost
\cite{hanneke2019value}; that off-target mass is actively harmful from
scratch is the surprising half---standard arguments say irrelevant data is
wasted, not harmful. Three observations support reading it causally: dataset
size and supervision density predict nothing (fit-free rank $\rho \le 0.27$
vs.\ $0.52$ for the rule; supplement), so ``more data'' is not the confound;
the flip survives dropping any generator family or any benchmark; and the
interventional grid of Sec.~\ref{sec:interv} reproduces the off-target cost
out-of-sample on data constructed after the analysis was frozen.

\noindent\textbf{Why this was invisible.}
Pooled across regimes the two directions cancel ($+0.6$ and $-0.2$ average to
mush), while symmetric metrics hedge---never best, never worst
(Table~\ref{tab:predictors}). This single sentence retroactively explains the
muddiness of direction-blind distribution matching in this space, including
our own earlier shared-ridge results and, we suspect, the mixed history of
``dataset distance'' signals more broadly.

\section{Selecting Training Data at the Reproducibility Ceiling}
\label{sec:predict}

The principle licenses a selection policy with zero fitted parameters:
charge each candidate the costs its regime actually pays,
\begin{equation}
\text{rank candidate training sets by } -D, \qquad
D =
\begin{cases}
\dtb + \dbt & \text{training from scratch,}\\[2pt]
\dbt & \text{pretrained feature backbone.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rule}
\end{equation}
No training runs on the target, no target labels, no learned weights; the
regime is known before training begins. Each arm is validated out-of-fold:
leave-one-variant-out selection of the pretrained direction is unanimous,
and leave-one-architecture-out refitting recovers the equal-weight scratch
arm on every fold (below). We also report the single-direction rule
($D{=}\dtb$ from scratch) as an ablation throughout; it is the right
\emph{design} objective (Sec.~\ref{sec:interv}) but the weaker
\emph{selection} score, because natural candidates vary in both costs.

Table~\ref{tab:predictors} compares the policy against seven alternatives,
all evaluated with identical within-context machinery. \emph{Matched
direction everywhere} is the single-direction rule. The two
\emph{fixed-direction} baselines apply one direction to every variant
regardless of regime. The \emph{symmetric} rows are regime-blind: the
mean-NN average (the scratch arm of the policy, applied indiscriminately)
and the distributional distances (FID, sliced Wasserstein-2, MMD in the same
BFV motion space). The \emph{13-feature motion ridge} is a learned baseline:
a ridge regression over the full motion descriptor set (both mean-NN
directions, multi-radius coverage in both directions, and directional
kNN-KL), fit on in-fold data and applied to held-out data---the natural
``just learn it'' alternative. The \emph{appearance ridge} is the identical
learned model over DINO appearance descriptors, the appearance control. The
policy ranks at $\rho = 0.52/0.52/0.50$ across held-out source / held-out
benchmark / both---best on every protocol, and uniform across them, which is
what one expects from a predictor with nothing to overfit. The orderings
beneath it are diagnostic: each fixed direction collapses on the regime
whose cost it mismatches; the regime-blind symmetric scores survive
everywhere but pay the forgiven-cost penalty in the pretrained cells; the
learned ridge, fit \emph{across} regimes, is diluted by exactly the
sign-flip this paper documents; and appearance carries no within-context
signal at all.

\noindent\textbf{Is $\rho\approx0.5$ good? It is approximately the ceiling.}
The correct reference is not $\rho{=}1$ but the reproducibility of the ground
truth itself. Transfer outcomes are noisy measurements: train a
\emph{different} variant of the same regime on the same sources and its
source ranking agrees with the first one's only at $\rho \approx
0.56$--$0.69$ (mean pairwise rank-agreement between same-regime variants; we
call this the \emph{empirical oracle}). No predictor can be validated beyond
this agreement, because the target itself does not agree with itself more
than this. Against it, the policy reaches a fraction of $1.08$ in the
backbone-pretrained regime ($0.612$ vs.\ oracle $0.564$) and $0.72$ from
scratch ($0.499$ vs.\ $0.698$; headroom concentrated in scratch GLU-Net). A
fraction slightly above $1$ does not mean the policy extracts information
retraining lacks---that would be impossible; it means the empirical oracle is
itself a noisy estimate built from single training runs, while the policy is
a deterministic function of the data and carries no run-to-run variance. The
honest statement is \emph{parity}: in the pretrained-backbone regime,
ranking by one distance is statistically indistinguishable from ranking by
actually retraining a sibling variant. Against a cross-architecture consensus
oracle (three architectures averaged, ceiling $0.732$), the policy recovers
$73$--$76\%$ across protocols: a static two-number descriptor retains most
of the information in training two other architectures and averaging
them.

\noindent\textbf{Two regimes, two tiny models.}
Fitting one 2-coefficient linear model per regime
(Table~\ref{tab:linear}) provides an independent, assumption-light test: the
scratch model puts weight on both directions; the pretrained model loads
dominantly on $\dbt$. A leave-one-architecture-out check separates what
transfers from what does not: refit on any two architectures, the scratch
weights stay two-sided---both costs retained---and rank the held-out
architecture's sources about as well as the symmetric average does
($\rho$ $+0.51$--$0.65$), while the
pretrained weights do \emph{not} transfer between the two backbone
architectures (held-out $+0.25$/$+0.46$, vs.\ the fit-free recall rule's
$+0.44$/$+0.67$ on the same contexts). The architecture-stable combiners are
therefore exactly the two the paper recommends: equal weights from scratch,
recall alone under a backbone. The two tiny models also unify everything
above into one
two-dimensional picture: the fit-free rule is this model with one weight set
to zero, and the symmetric average is this model with the weights forced
equal and regime-blind. Each special case wins exactly where its constraint
happens to be true---equal weights from scratch, a single dominant weight
under a backbone---and the per-regime model subsumes both. We also probed
for structure \emph{beyond} two linear weights per regime: a
precision$\times$recall interaction, anchor-level--conditioned weights,
lexicographic ``precision first, then recall'' hierarchies, a
worse-constraint-binds combiner, and a depth-2 gradient-boosted model.
None outperforms the two-coefficient fit held out (the boosted combiner is
strictly worse), so the combination frontier here is linear---and its
architecture-transferable points are exactly the policy's two components. Our practical
recommendation follows directly: \emph{rank by the summed distance if the
regime is unknown or training is from scratch (or, with reference runs, the
two-coefficient scratch model); rank by missing support $\dbt$ the moment a
pretrained backbone is in play.} That last clause is where directionality
earns its keep, and it is not a small correction: it is the difference
between $+0.39$ and $+0.61$ pooled, and between $+0.26$ and $+0.76$ on the
worst-served architecture. Table~\ref{tab:utility} scores this policy
directly, as decisions.

\noindent\textbf{What a practitioner gains.}
Table~\ref{tab:utility} and Fig.~\ref{fig:gap} translate ranking into
decisions, and they adjudicate the regime question at the level that
matters: picks. Used \emph{everywhere}, each ingredient splits the regimes
exactly as Sec.~\ref{sec:law} predicts---the summed distance picks better
from scratch, the matched direction picks far better under a backbone
(median pretrained regret $0.7$--$2.1$ vs.\ $3.0$; caption)---so the
regime-aware policy that combines them picks a source within a median
$1.2$--$2.8$ \pck{} of the oracle, versus $13.9$ for random selection;
appearance similarity is no better than random in two of three protocols.
Pairwise, the policy orders large-gap pairs correctly $77$--$78\%$ of the
time, matching the agreement of retraining a different architecture
($78\%$). Below a 2-\pck{} gap every strategy decays toward chance,
\emph{and so does retraining}: the residual errors are irreducible
measurement noise, not predictor deficiency.

\section{From Ranking to Absolute Prediction}
\label{sec:absolute}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/results/F5_absolute_scatter.png}
\caption{\textbf{Absolute \pck{} prediction} under the three protocols
(predictions $=$ anchor $+$ dispersion-calibrated policy score; colored by
regime; lines are decile-binned calibration curves). With anchors available
(left), predictions track the diagonal at MAE ${\approx}10$; with both
endpoints unseen (right), the anchor interpolates observed cells through
benchmark-similarity $\times$ source-similarity jointly and the policy
contributes ranking.}
\label{fig:abs}
\end{figure}

A rank correlation bounds how much \emph{variance} a predictor can explain:
if the policy's score were used as a linear predictor of within-context
transfer, its $R^2$ could not exceed roughly $\rho^2 \approx 0.25$---so even
a predictor at the reproducibility ceiling leaves ${\sim}75\%$ of
within-context variance unexplained, not because the predictor is weak but
because that variance is dominated by run-to-run training noise that no
dataset descriptor can see (Sec.~\ref{sec:predict}). Absolute accuracy
therefore comes from \emph{anchors}---observed runs that pin the scale---and
that division of labor is itself a finding: \textbf{anchors buy units; the
policy buys order}. Concretely
(Fig.~\ref{fig:abs}): with the target benchmark seen (held-out source),
cell-mean anchors plus the policy predict at MAE $9.3$, $r{=}0.88$; per-regime
gain calibration (one scale per regime, fit with the predicted source held
out) yields honestly-dispersed predictions with pretrained-regime MAE
$8.2$ and $r{=}0.90$---accurate enough to budget experiments against. With
\emph{both} endpoints unseen, a two-way kernel anchor (in-fold cells weighted
by benchmark-similarity $\times$ source-similarity) plus the
dispersion-calibrated policy reaches $r{\approx}0.5$ in absolute units while
preserving the policy's ranking---the residual magnitude beyond anchors is, per
our calibration-ceiling analysis, feature-unreachable. The supplement
details all anchor and calibration variants, including the failure modes.

Correlational principles should be stressed interventionally. Every result
so far shares one observational table; this section asks three questions of
data that table never touched---a procedurally generated grid

\noindent\textbf{(i) A pre-registered direction test: the off-target cost
is causal.}
Before training, we stated the prediction: off-target mass should rank
these never-seen sources from scratch. Table~\ref{tab:dirtest} reports the
result next to the variation that licenses it: the signal sits where
off-target mass has real dynamic range---FlyingThings ($3.1\times$ spread:
$+0.67$, against the coverage cost's $-0.38$); the KITTI cells are the
appearance-ablation twins of Table~\ref{tab:decomp} and cannot test a
motion cost (caption). This single-cell pass is the leakage-safe, causal
upgrade of Sec.~\ref{sec:law}'s strongest claim; re-evaluating the excluded
Middlebury benchmark with the repaired harness, and replicating with
GLU-Net/RAFT consumers, are the stated extensions
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). This is a \emph{design} result, not a selection
one: on natural pools, where both costs vary, the
summed score is the right from-scratch selector
(Tables~\ref{tab:predictors} and~\ref{tab:utility}); on designed data,
whose costs are decoupled, the generator must be moved along the binding
direction---the objective (ii) uses. Select with the policy; design with
the direction. Two scope notes: this test validates the off-target cost
only (a cost with little variation is causally invisible; its test is the
mirror grid, Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}), and its consumer is CATs++---the scratch
variant that leans hardest on off-target mass canonically
(Table~\ref{tab:law})---so replication with GLU-Net/RAFT consumers is the
stated generality check. The \emph{pretrained} arm cannot score its half of
the prediction: its transfer compresses into a band a few seed-widths wide
(3--8~\pck{} vs.\ 15--22 from scratch), so no ranking computed on it is
stable; its unstable point estimates nominally favor the unmatched
direction ($\dtb$ $+0.64$, $\dbt$ $+0.21$, sym $+0.37$), which we report
rather than discount---under either reading, the pretrained half rests on
the canonical data until a test with pretrained-regime dynamic range exists
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

\noindent\textbf{(ii) Closed-loop generation (predicted gains realize).}
A render-free search over generator motion parameters---scored only by
motion match to the target, no training in the loop---produced a tuned source
whose trained margin over the anchor matched prediction on KITTI-2015
($+0.93$ predicted, $+0.87$ realized), while a FlyingThings null control
(predicted ${\approx}0$) realized $-1.6$: no false positive. Selection costs
geometry decoding only; the photorealistic render pass is skipped during
search.

\noindent\textbf{Appearance vs.\ motion, decomposed.}
The grid's controllability lets us cross the two axes a natural dataset

plausible motion the two axes are the same size; we therefore rest the claim on
specificity, not dominance.) Realism is not the targeting axis; matched motion
is.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

We state the principle's evidentiary status precisely. \emph{It is a regime
tendency, not a per-variant law}: 8 of 9 variants flip as predicted, but
scratch GLU-Net is direction-neutral (its flip CI straddles zero), and the
exact permutation $p{=}0.0286$ is the floor of an eight-unit test---small-$n$
by construction. \emph{Its two halves are unevenly validated}: the
from-scratch half passed a pre-registered out-of-sample test on a controlled
grid; the pretrained half rests entirely on the observational table, because
the grid's pretrained arm compresses into a seed-noise band---its unstable
point estimates nominally favor the unmatched direction, and we report them
undiscounted in Sec.~\ref{sec:interv} rather than omit them. A
decisive pretrained test needs a grid with pretrained-regime dynamic range,
or a new architecture; the two-cost reading makes three falsifiable
predictions we consider most informative, stated here in advance for a
FlowFormer-class model (pretrained Twins-SVT encoder): its from-scratch
cells should be ranked by the summed distance, its pretrained cells by
missing support alone, and per-regime weights fitted on our three
architectures should transfer to it without improving on those two fit-free
scores. \emph{The scratch missing-support cost has no interventional test
yet}: on the existing grid the off-target cost carries the dynamic range
(and the KITTI cells are precision-matched by design), so it validates only
that cost; the mirror grid---sources searched to vary coverage at fixed
off-target mass---is the experiment that would test it directly, and our
render-free search infrastructure supports constructing one. \emph{The
pretrained evidence is thin in a second way}: only two backbone
architectures exist in our study, and fitted pretrained weights already
fail to transfer between them (which is why the policy's pretrained arm is
fit-free); when the regime is unknown, the summed distance is the safe
default. Ranking at
$\rho{\approx}0.5$ caps within-context explainable variance at
${\sim}25\%$; absolute accuracy beyond anchors is feature-unreachable in our
analysis. Semantic correspondence (SPair) is weakly predicted---motion
descriptors do not capture semantic compatibility. One benchmark
(Middlebury) was excluded after we identified an evaluation-harness defect
during analysis; all reported numbers use the remaining nine (the supplement
documents the exclusion and a sensitivity analysis).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Which synthetic dataset is best is not a property of the dataset: it is a
property of the dataset \emph{and the regime that will consume it}, because
the regime sets which mismatch costs the model pays. Training from scratch
pays for both off-target mass and missing support---so candidates are ranked
by their summed directed distance---while a pretrained backbone forgives the
first cost and leaves the second, and because the two costs trade off across
real generators, charging the forgiven one silently corrupts the ranking.
This two-cost structure ranks training data at the reproducibility ceiling
of transfer itself, where the identical appearance-space measurement carries
nothing; it predicts absolute performance where anchors exist; and it splits
cleanly into two uses---\emph{select} natural candidates with the
regime-priced total cost, \emph{design} generated data by moving the
regime's binding direction, the move our controlled grid validates
out-of-sample. Because the signal is a property of the data rather than the
renderer, the same policy guides two structurally different generators---a
3D scene simulator and a procedural implicit-surface generator---which
suggests it is a generator-agnostic layer for deciding \emph{what} to
synthesize, not a property of any one pipeline. While the field invests
heavily in rendering data more realistically and more cheaply, we offer a
complementary and largely orthogonal tool: a target-conditioned answer to
what that data should contain, for correspondence and plausibly for other
dense tasks where training and target distributions can be compared. The
recipe is correspondingly simple: before improving how you render, measure
$\dtb$ and $\dbt$ against your target, and charge your candidates the costs
your training regime will actually pay.
