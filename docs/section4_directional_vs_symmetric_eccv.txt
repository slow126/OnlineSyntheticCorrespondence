\section{Comparing Datasets: Directional vs. Symmetric Distances}
\label{sec:directional_vs_symmetric}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
To predict transfer performance, we need a discrepancy measure that distinguishes \emph{where} mismatch occurs.
In particular, we separate two failure modes: (i) \emph{under-coverage}, where the evaluation set contains regions not supported by train, and (ii) \emph{extra mass}, where train contains regions irrelevant to evaluation.
This section formalizes directional discrepancies, contrasts normalization variants, and explains why symmetric baselines (e.g., MMD) can collapse these modes.

\subsection{Directed discrepancy}
\label{subsec:directed_discrepancy}

Let $T=\{t_i\}_{i=1}^{n_T}$ be train vectors and $E=\{e_j\}_{j=1}^{n_E}$ evaluation vectors in a shared feature space.
We compute directed nearest-neighbor (NN) distances:
\begin{equation}
d_{E\rightarrow T}(j)=\min_i \|e_j-t_i\|_2,\qquad
d_{T\rightarrow E}(i)=\min_j \|t_i-e_j\|_2.
\end{equation}
From these, we use directional statistics (mean/median/p90) and directional coverage at threshold $\epsilon$:
\begin{equation}
C_{E\rightarrow T}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{n_E}\sum_{j=1}^{n_E}\mathbb{1}[d_{E\rightarrow T}(j)\le\epsilon],\quad
C_{T\rightarrow E}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{n_T}\sum_{i=1}^{n_T}\mathbb{1}[d_{T\rightarrow E}(i)\le\epsilon].
\end{equation}
Intuitively, $E\rightarrow T$ measures missing support, while $T\rightarrow E$ measures extra mass.

In our primary flow pipeline, we use fixed-$\epsilon$ directional curves (``raw epsilon''), which avoids radius-division artifacts and preserves direct geometric interpretability.

\subsection{Normalization variants: qnorm/rnorm}
\label{subsec:qnorm_rnorm}

When self-radii are available, we evaluate two normalizations:
\begin{equation}
C^{\text{qnorm}}_{E\rightarrow T}
=\frac{1}{n_E}\sum_j\mathbb{1}[d_{E\rightarrow T}(j)\le R_E],\qquad
C^{\text{rnorm}}_{E\rightarrow T}
=\frac{1}{n_E}\sum_j\mathbb{1}[d_{E\rightarrow T}(j)\le R_T],
\end{equation}
and analogously for $T\rightarrow E$.
qnorm uses the query-set radius, while rnorm uses the reference-set radius.

We also considered ratio features such as
\texttt{train\_to\_eval\_mean\_dist\_over\_radius\_eval}
and
\texttt{eval\_to\_train\_mean\_dist\_over\_radius\_train}.
Empirically, these can become heavy-tailed when radii are small, which makes optimization and ranking less stable.
This behavior is consistent with denominator sensitivity in ratio statistics.

\subsection{Symmetric baseline: MMD}
\label{subsec:mmd_baseline}

As symmetric baseline, we use Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)~\cite{gretton2012kernel}:
\begin{equation}
\text{MMD}^2(T,E)=
\mathbb{E}_{t,t'}k(t,t')+
\mathbb{E}_{e,e'}k(e,e')-
2\mathbb{E}_{t,e}k(t,e),
\end{equation}
with kernel $k(\cdot,\cdot)$.
By construction, $\text{MMD}(T,E)=\text{MMD}(E,T)$; therefore it cannot encode direction.
Two dataset pairs with opposite directional failure patterns may have similar MMD, despite very different transfer implications.

\subsection{Empirical behavior in our runs}
\label{subsec:empirical_behavior}

Our strongest mixed setting combines directional flow epsilon predictors, directional DINO KL, HOF density, and MMD:
\begin{center}
\small
\texttt{leakage\_free\_combo\_flow\_eps\_raw\_\_dino\_kl\_k5\_\_hof\_density\_l2\_\_mmd}.
\end{center}
This run attains LOTO rank Spearman $0.231$ with regret $4.57$, indicating useful ranking signal under strict leave-one-train-out evaluation.

In contrast, a radius-normalized manifold variant
(\texttt{leakage\_free\_flow\_joint\_kmeans\_manifold})
shows substantially weaker ranking behavior (LOTO rank Spearman $-0.17$, regret $52.91$), even though predictive correlation on raw targets remains moderate.
This supports our use of raw directional distances as primary features.

KL-only runs are informative but unstable across settings.
For example, flow-KL-only and HOF-KL-only runs exhibit poor LOTO rank behavior (negative rank Spearman in our sweep), consistent with known finite-sample variance of kNN density-ratio estimators~\cite{kozachenko1987sample,perez2008kullback}.
We therefore treat KL as a diagnostic component rather than the primary discrepancy signal.

\subsection{Conceptual failure-mode separation}
\label{subsec:conceptual_figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:directional_vs_symmetric_concept} illustrates the key point.
Case A (under-coverage) and Case B (extra mass) swap directional distances, while symmetric MMD stays nearly unchanged.
Hence, directional metrics are necessary to disambiguate mismatch modes.

\paragraph{Takeaway.}
For transfer-oriented dataset comparison, we recommend reporting both directions explicitly and using directional discrepancy as the primary signal.
Symmetric distances are useful as complementary summaries, but insufficient on their own to resolve under-coverage versus extra-mass mismatch.
